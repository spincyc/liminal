# Drill Protocols

How to run a practice session so it produces learning rather than activity.

Every study plan in this directory assumes you're using these protocols. They
matter more than the schedule.

---

## The session template

45-75 minutes. Adjust the block sizes, not the order.

| Minutes | Block | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0-5 | **Warm-up** — 5 easy items in a skill you have | Get into it without frustration |
| 5-15 | **Missed-items review** — the app's Missed mode | Spaced repetition on old errors |
| 15-25 | **Learn** — read one guide section | New material, while fresh |
| 25-50 | **Drill** — 15-25 items in that skill | Build the procedure |
| 50-70 | **Review** — every miss, plus every flagged item | Where the learning happens |

**When you're short on time, cut the drill block, not the review block.**
Reviewing 8 items properly beats attempting 30 carelessly.

## The four drill modes

### 1. Untimed accuracy drill

**When:** learning a new skill, or fixing a process error.

**How:** no clock. Write every step. Aim for correctness, not speed.

**Volume:** 10-15 items.

**Rule:** if you're unsure, work it out fully rather than guessing. The point is
to build a correct procedure.

Speed built on a broken method just produces faster errors. Always start here.

### 2. Timed skill drill

**When:** the procedure is solid and you need it under pressure.

**How:** set a per-question target and hold to it.

| Section | Target per question |
| --- | --- |
| SAT RW — conventions | 35 sec |
| SAT RW — reading | 75 sec |
| SAT Math | 90 sec |
| ACT English — conventions | 22 sec |
| ACT English — rhetorical | 50 sec |
| ACT Math | 65 sec |
| ACT Reading | 60 sec |
| ACT Science | 55 sec |

**Volume:** 15-25 items.

Note that these targets are slightly *faster* than the section averages. Train
below the required pace so real timing feels comfortable.

### 3. Mixed-mode drill

**When:** two weeks or less from the test, or when individual skills are solid
but you're missing things in full sections.

**How:** full-mix mode, all domains, real timing.

**Why it matters:** the hardest part of a real section isn't any single skill —
it's **recognizing which skill applies**. Isolated drilling doesn't train that.
Students who drill only by domain often plateau because they've never practiced
identification.

**Volume:** 25-40 items.

### 4. Mini test

**When:** every one to two weeks, as a check-in between full practice tests.

**How:** 20 questions, real clock, no feedback until the end, then review all
20. See [Mini tests](../09-mini-tests.md).

**Why it is its own mode:** every other drill gives you feedback as you go,
which is right for learning and wrong for measuring. A mini test is the only
short format that tells you what you can do unaided.

### 5. Missed-items drill

**When:** every session, in the first 15 minutes.

**How:** the app's Missed mode. Redo previously wrong items.

**Volume:** 10-15 items.

**Rule:** if you miss it again, it goes back to the start of the spacing
schedule and you rewrite your rule for it. A repeated miss means your original
diagnosis was wrong.

## Choosing difficulty

**Practice where you get 60-75% correct.**

| Accuracy | Meaning | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Below 50% | Guessing, learning nothing | Drop a difficulty level |
| 60-75% | The productive zone | Stay here |
| Above 85% | Rehearsing what you know | Move up a level |

**Progression for a new skill:**

1. Easy, untimed — build the procedure (10-15 items)
2. Medium, untimed — apply it to harder cases (15 items)
3. Medium, timed — build speed (20 items)
4. Hard, untimed — stretch (10-15 items)
5. Mixed difficulty, timed — consolidate (20 items)

Don't skip step 1 because it feels beneath you. The point isn't the difficulty,
it's establishing the procedure you'll use at every level.

## The review protocol

The most important 20 minutes of any session.

**For each missed item, 2-4 minutes:**

1. **Redo it covered**, before reading the explanation. This tells you the error
   type.
2. **Read the full explanation**, even if you now understand — it may show a
   faster method.
3. **Read why each other choice is wrong.** This is where you learn the item
   writer's toolkit.
4. **Classify** the error: content, process, careless, or time.
5. **Write one sentence** describing what you'll do differently.
6. **Schedule a redo** for three days out.

**Also review items you got right** if you guessed, were down to two, took too
long, or used a method you couldn't repeat. Flag these during the drill, not
after — you'll forget which ones they were.

### The one-sentence rule

Step 5 is the deliverable. Over a full plan you accumulate 30-60 sentences
describing *your* specific failure modes.

That list is the single most valuable thing to read the day before your test.
Keep it in one file.

**If you do nothing else from this document, do step 5.** Fifteen seconds per
miss. It works.

## Spacing schedule

| Redo | When | Tests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | 3 days later | Did the fix take |
| 2nd | 10 days later | Is it durable |
| 3rd | 3-4 weeks later | Is it automatic |

Never redo the same day — you'll remember the answer rather than the method.

---

## Weekly audit — 20 minutes

Once a week, look at your log in aggregate rather than item by item.

**1. Count error types.** Which dominates? That determines next week's *kind* of
work. See [Diagnostic routing](diagnostic-routing.md).

**2. Count by skill.** Which two or three appear most? Those are next week's
targets. Ignore skills that appear once.

**3. Reread your rules list.** Are you still breaking rules you wrote three
weeks ago? Promote those to a "top five" list you read before every session.

**4. Adjust the plan.** If a targeted skill improved, drop it and pick up the
next one. If it didn't, re-diagnose — a persistent "content" gap is usually a
process problem underneath.

## Full-length practice tests

**Minimum 3, maximum about 6** before test day.

**Conditions matter:**

- Saturday morning, at the real start time
- One sitting, real timing, real breaks
- Phone off and in another room
- Bluebook for the SAT; paper and a real bubble sheet for the ACT

Score inflation from taking a test on your couch in three sittings is real, and
it will hurt on test day.

**Spacing:** roughly every 2-3 weeks. Last full test **7-10 days before** the
real one, never the day before.

**Reviewing a full test:**

- **Same day, 30 minutes:** while it's fresh, note where you felt rushed, where
  you lost focus, which section drained you.
- **Next day, 2-3 hours:** full item-by-item review.
- **Then extract exactly three things:** two or three skills to drill, one
  pacing adjustment, one mechanical habit. Not fifteen. Fifteen improvements
  produce zero improvements.

## Using the app's session modes

| Mode | Use it for |
| --- | --- |
| **Targeted** | Domain and skill drilling — the core of every plan |
| **Full mix** | Mixed-mode practice, final weeks |
| **Recommended** | When you're not sure what to work on |
| **Missed** | The first 15 minutes of every session |
| **Bookmarked** | Items you want to return to deliberately |
| **Flagged** | Items you got right but weren't sure about |

**Flag aggressively during drills.** A right answer for the wrong reason is a
future wrong answer, and flagging is the only way you'll find it later.

## Common protocol failures

| Failure | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| **Skipping review** | You rehearse errors instead of fixing them |
| **Reading explanations and nodding** | Recognition, not recall. Reproduce it later. |
| **Only reviewing hard questions** | Missed easy questions are guaranteed leaked points |
| **Batch-reviewing at week's end** | You've forgotten your reasoning — the most diagnostic data |
| **Writing nothing down** | "I'll remember" is false |
| **Only timed practice** | Locks in bad habits at higher speed |
| **Only untimed practice** | Never builds selection and triage |
| **Only domain drilling** | Never trains skill *identification* |
| **Too many full tests** | Burns material and energy for diminishing returns |
| **Dismissing careless errors** | They are your score |
