# Choosing Between the SAT and the ACT

Every US college that considers test scores accepts both, and none prefers one.
There is no strategic advantage to either in admissions. The only question is
which one *you* score better on relative to other students.

For most students the difference is small. For a meaningful minority it is
large — 3-4 ACT points or 100+ SAT points — and those students are leaving real
score on the table if they pick wrong.

---

## The short version

Take a timed practice section of each. Compare using the concordance table
below. If they're close, take whichever you preferred; if one is clearly better,
commit to it and stop splitting your prep.

If you have already taken both officially, you already have your answer. Use it.

## Structural differences that decide it

### Time pressure

This is the biggest single differentiator.

| | SAT | ACT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RW / English | ~71 sec per question | 42 sec per question |
| Math | ~95 sec per question | 67 sec per question |
| Reading | (combined with writing) | 67 sec per question |

**The ACT is a speed test.** Its content is generally more approachable, but you
have far less time to think. The SAT gives you room to work through harder
material.

- If you are a **fast, decisive worker** who gets bored and loses focus with
  time to spare → ACT.
- If you are **careful and thorough** but slow, and your practice-test misses
  are concentrated at the end of sections → SAT.

This one factor explains most large gaps between a student's two scores.

### Science

The ACT has an optional Science section; the SAT has none. Because it's optional
and outside the Composite, it no longer forces the choice the way it used to.
But note that ACT Science is not really a science-knowledge test — it is a
speed-reading-charts test. Students who love biology sometimes hate it, and
students who have never taken physics often do fine.

### Math content and weighting

| | SAT | ACT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Share of total score | 50% | 33% (one of three) |
| Formula sheet | Provided | **None** |
| Calculator | Whole section, Desmos built in | Whole section |
| Emphasis | Algebra and data analysis heavy | Broader, includes more geometry |
| Trigonometry | Light — right triangles mostly | More, including identities and graphs |
| Grid-ins | ~25% of questions | None; all multiple choice |

**Math weighting is a real strategic factor.** SAT Math is half your score. On
the ACT it is one of three components. If math is your weakness, the ACT dilutes
it; if math is your strength, the SAT amplifies it.

The ACT also reaches further into geometry and trigonometry, and gives you no
formula sheet. Students who are good at memorizing and pattern-recognizing tend
to like ACT Math. Students who reason well but forget formulas prefer the SAT.

### Reading

| | SAT | ACT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Passage length | 25-150 words, one question each | Long passages, several questions each |
| Total reading volume | Lower | Higher |
| Question style | Discrete, analytical, evidence-focused | Detail-location heavy, faster |
| Vocabulary | Words in context, moderately hard | Lighter |
| Poetry / literary | Some literary prose | Prose fiction/literary narrative passage |

The SAT's short-passage format suits students who lose the thread in long texts
or who read slowly but analyze well. The ACT's long-passage format suits fluent,
fast readers who can hold a whole argument in mind and locate details quickly.

### Adaptivity

The SAT adapts between modules; the ACT does not. Some students find adaptivity
stressful — the second module getting harder feels like punishment even though
it's a good sign. Others prefer the shorter total test.

## A decision checklist

Score each row honestly. Mostly left column → SAT. Mostly right → ACT.

| Question | Points to SAT | Points to ACT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Do you run out of time on practice sections? | Yes | No |
| Do you read long passages comfortably and quickly? | No | Yes |
| Is math your strongest subject? | Yes | No |
| Is math your weakest subject? | No | Yes |
| Do you remember formulas easily? | No | Yes |
| Are you comfortable with geometry and trigonometry? | No | Yes |
| Do you like working on a computer? | Yes | No |
| Do you prefer being able to write on the page? | No | Yes |
| Do you handle charts and data displays quickly? | Either | Yes |
| Do you get flustered when a test gets harder mid-way? | No | Yes |

## Should you take both?

**Usually no.** Prep splits, and two mediocre scores beat neither one good one.
Pick a test after a comparison, then commit.

**Take both when:**

- Your practice scores are genuinely equivalent and you cannot decide. In that
  case, sit both once officially and let real scores decide, then retake the
  better one.
- You have already taken one, scored below your target, and suspect the format
  was the problem rather than your preparation.
- You have lots of runway — a full year — and prep time is not scarce.

**Do not take both when** your test date is under two months out. Split
attention at that range costs more than the information is worth.

### The "take each once more" case

If you have already sat both and are retaking before applications, the logic is
different and simpler:

1. Look at your two scores through the concordance table. Are they equivalent,
   or is one clearly stronger?
2. If one is clearly stronger, **retake only that one** and put all your prep
   into it. A second retake of your weaker test is usually wasted money.
3. If they're equivalent, retaking both is defensible — but only if you have
   time to prepare for both formats without diluting either. Sequence them at
   least two weeks apart and prep for them in blocks, not simultaneously.
4. Either way, check superscoring first. If your schools superscore, a retake
   can only help, which makes the decision low-risk.

## Test-optional policies

Many colleges are test-optional or test-blind. Two things worth knowing:

- **Test-optional does not mean tests don't help.** At most test-optional
  schools, a score at or above the school's median strengthens an application. A
  score well below it is better omitted.
- **Merit scholarships frequently still use scores**, even at test-optional
  schools, and so do honors colleges and some state grant programs. This is the
  most common reason a test-optional applicant should still test.

Rule of thumb: submit if your score is at or above the middle of a school's
published range; consider omitting if it is below the 25th percentile. Check
each school's published range rather than guessing.
