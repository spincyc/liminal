# Timing and Pacing

Most students who miss their target score do not lack knowledge. They run out of
time, rush the back half, and make errors they would never make untimed.

Pacing is a trainable skill with a specific method. This file is that method.

---

## The budgets

### Digital SAT

| Module | Questions | Time | Per question |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Reading and Writing (each of 2) | 27 | 32 min | **71 sec** |
| Math (each of 2) | 22 | 35 min | **95 sec** |

### Enhanced ACT

| Section | Questions | Time | Per question |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| English | 50 | 35 min | **42 sec** |
| Mathematics | 45 | 50 min | **67 sec** |
| Reading | 36 | 40 min | **67 sec** |
| Science | 40 | 40 min | **60 sec** |

Averages are misleading on their own. You should be spending **well under** the
average on easy items so you can spend well over it on hard ones. A student who
takes exactly 67 seconds on every ACT Math question is doing it wrong in both
directions.

## Checkpoints — the only pacing tool you need

Don't track per-question timing during the test; it's impossible and it's
stressful. Track **three or four checkpoints** per section and adjust.

Memorize the checkpoints for your test. Write them on your scratch paper in the
first ten seconds of the section.

### SAT Reading and Writing module (32 min, 27 questions)

| At | You should be at |
| --- | --- |
| 8 min elapsed | Q7 |
| 16 min elapsed | Q14 |
| 24 min elapsed | Q21 |
| 30 min elapsed | Q27, review flagged |

### SAT Math module (35 min, 22 questions)

| At | You should be at |
| --- | --- |
| 9 min elapsed | Q6 |
| 18 min elapsed | Q12 |
| 26 min elapsed | Q18 |
| 33 min elapsed | Q22, review flagged |

### ACT English (35 min, 50 questions, ~5 passages)

| At | You should be at |
| --- | --- |
| 7 min | End of passage 1 (Q10) |
| 14 min | End of passage 2 (Q20) |
| 21 min | End of passage 3 (Q30) |
| 28 min | End of passage 4 (Q40) |
| 35 min | Done |

Seven minutes per passage. This is the cleanest pacing structure on either test
— use it.

### ACT Mathematics (50 min, 45 questions)

| At | You should be at |
| --- | --- |
| 12 min | Q15 |
| 25 min | Q28 |
| 38 min | Q38 |
| 50 min | Done |

Note the deliberate front-loading: 15 questions in the first 12 minutes, but
only 7 in the last 12. ACT Math is ordered roughly easy to hard, so bank time
early. If you're at Q15 at 18 minutes, you are in trouble and should speed up
immediately, not gradually.

### ACT Reading (40 min, 36 questions, 4 passages)

**10 minutes per passage.** Approximately 3 minutes reading, 7 minutes on
questions.

| At | You should be at |
| --- | --- |
| 10 min | End of passage 1 |
| 20 min | End of passage 2 |
| 30 min | End of passage 3 |
| 40 min | Done |

Hard stop at 10 minutes per passage. Leaving three questions on passage 4 to
perfect two on passage 2 is a losing trade — every question is worth the same.

### ACT Science (40 min, 40 questions, ~6-7 passages)

**Roughly 5-6 minutes per passage.** Conflicting Viewpoints takes longer than
the data passages; budget about 7 minutes for it and 5 for the others.

| At | You should be at |
| --- | --- |
| 10 min | 2 passages done |
| 20 min | 3-4 passages done |
| 30 min | 5 passages done |
| 40 min | Done |

## The two-pass method

Never let one hard question eat four questions' worth of time. Instead:

### Pass 1 — sweep

Go through the section in order. For each question, make a fast call:

- **Easy / clear method** → do it now, carefully
- **Hard but doable** → attempt, but if you're 30 seconds in with no traction,
  mark it and move
- **No idea where to start** → mark it, guess a default letter immediately, move
  on within 10 seconds

Never leave it blank when you skip. Put your default answer down *now*, in case
you never return.

### Pass 2 — return

Come back to marked questions with the pressure off. You'll find that some of
them are suddenly obvious — this is real, not superstition. Removing time
pressure and having seen related questions in between genuinely helps.

### Pass 3 — sanity check

With 2-3 minutes left, verify:

- Every question has an answer (on paper: no bubbling gaps or misalignment)
- Grid-in formats are legal (SAT)
- No obviously mis-transcribed answers

### The 30-second rule

If you've spent 30 seconds and don't know how to *start*, you are not going to
finish it efficiently. Mark, guess, move. This is the single most valuable
pacing habit and it feels terrible until you've practiced it.

The instinct to persist is expensive. Two minutes on one hard question costs you
roughly three easy questions elsewhere.

## Adaptations by section

### SAT Reading and Writing

Questions are grouped by domain in a fixed order: **Craft and Structure →
Information and Ideas → Standard English Conventions → Expression of Ideas**.

Two strategies exploit this:

**Strategy A — in order.** Simple, and fine if your pacing is solid.

**Strategy B — conventions first.** Grammar questions (Standard English
Conventions) are usually the fastest points on the module — often 30-40 seconds
each. Some students jump to them first, bank time, then return to the reading
questions. Within a module you can navigate freely, so this is entirely
permitted.

Strategy B is worth testing in practice. It helps students who tend to run out
of time, and hurts students who get disoriented jumping around. Try both and
keep what works.

### SAT Math

Roughly ordered easy to hard within each module. The last 4-5 questions of a
module are the hardest.

If you're not going to finish, the correct sacrifice is **the last few
questions, not the middle ones**. Never rush questions 8-15 in order to reach
question 22 — those middle questions are far more likely to be gettable.

**Use Desmos aggressively for speed**, not just for hard problems. Graphing a
system of equations is often faster than solving it by hand, and it removes
arithmetic-error risk. See the
[Desmos playbook](sat/math/06-desmos-playbook.md).

### ACT English — the fastest section

42 seconds per question sounds brutal but isn't, because most items are short
grammar questions answerable in 15-20 seconds. The time goes to the handful of
rhetorical questions asking about the passage as a whole.

- Do **not** read the passage first. Read from the start of the sentence
  containing the underlined portion, through the end of that sentence.
- For questions about paragraph placement or the passage's overall goal, you
  need broader context — those are the ones to spend on.
- Questions asking "should the writer add this sentence" require reading the
  surrounding two sentences, not the whole passage.

Target: finish with 2-3 minutes left. This is very achievable and it's where
struggling ACT students find their biggest gains.

### ACT Reading — the hardest to pace

Four passages, 10 minutes each, hard stop.

**Passage order:** you do not have to go in order. The standard order is
Literary Narrative, Social Science, Humanities, Natural Science. If one type is
reliably your worst, do it last so that if anything gets shortchanged, it's the
passage you'd score lowest on anyway.

**Reading approach** — three viable methods, and you should pick one in
practice, not on test day:

| Method | How | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Read then answer** | Skim the passage in ~3 min for structure and main idea, then answer | Fast readers |
| **Skim then hunt** | Read first and last paragraph plus first sentences, then locate details per question | Slow readers, detail-heavy passages |
| **Questions first** | Read the questions, then read hunting for those specifics | Students who lose focus reading cold |

None is universally best. "Read then answer" wins for most strong readers;
"skim then hunt" rescues students who can't finish.

### ACT Science

The dominant time sink is reading the passage text when you don't need to.

- Go **straight to the questions** for data-representation and
  research-summaries passages. Most questions send you to a specific figure.
- Read the intro text only when a question requires it — typically for
  experimental-design and evaluation questions.
- **Conflicting Viewpoints must actually be read.** Budget extra for it. Many
  students do it last for this reason.

## Building pacing as a skill

You cannot fix pacing on test day. Train it:

**Weeks out:** practice untimed to build correct procedures. Speed built on a
broken method just produces faster errors.

**Then:** practice with generous timing — 125% of real time. Get accurate at a
comfortable pace.

**Then:** practice at exactly real time. Use checkpoints. Note where you fall
behind.

**Occasionally:** practice at 90% of real time. Compressed practice makes real
timing feel roomy, and it forces the triage habit.

### Diagnosing a pacing problem

Look at *where* your misses cluster:

| Pattern | Diagnosis | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Misses cluster at the end | Classic pacing failure | Checkpoints, 30-second rule |
| Misses spread evenly | Content or process problem, not timing | Content review |
| Misses cluster at the start | Slow warm-up, or anxiety | Warm-up items before the test |
| Finish early with misses everywhere | Rushing, careless errors | Slow down; use the extra time |

That last row is real and underdiagnosed. Finishing an ACT section with eight
minutes left and 6 misses means you should be going *slower*.

## Test-day timing mechanics

- **Wear a watch** (analog or basic digital, no smart features, no audible
  alarm). Do not rely on the room clock or the proctor.
- **Write your checkpoints down** at the start of each section.
- **Check the clock at checkpoints only.** Constant clock-watching costs real
  time and raises anxiety.
- **On the SAT**, Bluebook shows a countdown timer you can hide. Most students
  do better hiding it and revealing it at checkpoints.
- **Five-minute warning:** stop starting new hard questions. Sweep for blanks,
  verify formats, fill in defaults.

## The mental part

Two rules that matter more than any of the above:

**One question is worth one point.** The hardest question on the test is worth
exactly what the easiest one is. Every minute spent on a question you probably
won't get is stolen from questions you definitely would have.

**Do not carry a bad section.** Sections are scored separately. A disastrous
Math module has zero effect on your Reading score unless you let it wreck your
focus. The break exists for this. Use it to reset, not to relitigate.
