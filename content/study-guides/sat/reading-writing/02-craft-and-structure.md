# SAT Reading and Writing — Craft and Structure

**Catalog domain:** Craft and Structure
**Skills:** Words in Context · Text Structure and Purpose · Cross-Text Connections
**Roughly 28% of the section** — about 15 of 54 questions, the largest domain

These questions open each module. They're about *how* a text works rather than
what it says.

---

## Words in Context

The single most common question type on the section — typically 6-8 per module.
It is also one of the most trainable.

### What the test asks

- "As used in the text, what does the word *[X]* most nearly mean?"
- "Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or
  phrase?"

Two directions of the same skill: read a word and identify its meaning, or read
a gap and supply the right word.

### This is not a vocabulary test

It is a **context** test. The words are usually common, and the tested meaning
is frequently *not* the most familiar one. Knowing a definition is not enough
and sometimes actively hurts — the familiar meaning is regularly the trap.

### Method

1. **Cover the answer choices.** Physically or mentally. This is not optional;
   it's the technique.
2. Read the full sentence, plus the one before and after.
3. **Insert your own word** in the blank. Any word that fits. Don't strain for
   sophistication — "good," "bad," "big," "cause" are fine placeholders.
4. Uncover the choices. Find the one closest to your word.
5. **Plug it back in** and read the sentence aloud in your head. Does it work
   grammatically *and* logically?

Step 1 is the whole method. Every distractor is engineered to sound plausible
in isolation. A prediction makes you immune to that.

### Finding the context clue

Every one of these questions contains a clue. Find it before predicting.

| Clue type | Signal | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Definition** | Appositive, `that is`, `in other words`, dashes | The meaning is stated nearby |
| **Contrast** | `but`, `however`, `unlike`, `whereas`, `rather than`, `despite` | The word is *opposite* to something nearby |
| **Continuation** | `and`, `moreover`, `similarly`, `likewise`, `indeed` | The word is *similar* to something nearby |
| **Cause/effect** | `because`, `therefore`, `as a result`, `since` | The word fits a causal chain |
| **Example** | `for instance`, `such as`, `including` | The examples define the category |

Contrast clues are the highest-yield. If you see `but` or `however` near the
blank, the answer is roughly the opposite of the nearby idea, and you can often
answer without fully understanding the passage.

### Patterns

**The familiar meaning is often wrong.** If a common word is being tested, ask
why. Words like *appreciate* (increase in value), *qualify* (limit), *arrest*
(stop), *novel* (new), *sound* (valid), *just* (fair), *check* (restrain),
*compromise* (weaken) carry secondary meanings the SAT loves.

**Register matters.** Passages are formal, so casual choices are usually wrong
even when the denotation fits.

**Connotation matters.** Two words can mean roughly the same thing with opposite
emotional loading. *Frugal* vs. *stingy*. *Confident* vs. *arrogant*. *Ambitious*
vs. *grasping*. Match the passage's attitude.

**Precision matters.** When two choices both fit, the more precise one wins.
"Suggests" and "demonstrates" both fit many blanks; only one matches the
strength of the surrounding claim.

**Beware the word that matches the topic.** In a passage about economics, a
choice that's an economics term feels right. That's a manufactured feeling.

### Trap types

| Trap | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Common meaning** | The word's usual sense, not the contextual one |
| **Right idea, wrong strength** | `enormous` where `noticeable` is meant |
| **Right idea, wrong connotation** | Positive word where the tone is critical |
| **Topic word** | Sounds like the subject matter, doesn't fit the sentence |
| **Grammatically wrong** | Doesn't take the same preposition or part of speech |

That last one is a free elimination people forget: if the sentence is *"the
result [blank] with earlier findings"* and a choice can't be followed by *with*,
it's dead regardless of meaning.

### High-value word list

Words that recur in SAT-style academic prose, grouped by function.

**Describing arguments and claims:**
assert · posit · contend · maintain · concede · refute · substantiate ·
corroborate · undermine · qualify · reconcile · attribute · attest · repudiate

**Describing degree and certainty:**
tentative · provisional · definitive · categorical · nominal · marginal ·
substantial · negligible · pervasive · ubiquitous · sporadic · unequivocal

**Describing attitude:**
skeptical · ambivalent · dismissive · deferential · candid · reticent ·
indifferent · earnest · wry · measured · circumspect · disparaging

**Describing change:**
augment · diminish · attenuate · exacerbate · mitigate · precipitate ·
stagnate · proliferate · curtail · supplant · consolidate

**Describing relationships:**
analogous · disparate · contingent · concomitant · antithetical · tangential ·
integral · reciprocal · discrete

Learn these as *usable* words — able to be defined and used in a sentence — not
as flashcard trivia. But note: memorizing vocabulary is a low-yield activity
compared to practicing the prediction method. Do the method work first.

## Text Structure and Purpose

### What the test asks

- "Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the
  text as a whole?"
- "Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?"
- "What is the main purpose of the text?"

### Function questions

You're asked what a sentence *does*, not what it says. This distinction is the
entire question, and it's where students lose these.

Common functions, and the vocabulary used to describe them:

| Function | Typical phrasing in answers |
| --- | --- |
| Introduce a topic | "introduce a phenomenon that the text goes on to explain" |
| Present a common view | "describe a widely held assumption" |
| Raise an objection | "identify a limitation of the approach described" |
| Provide evidence | "offer data supporting the claim in the previous sentence" |
| Give an example | "illustrate the concept introduced earlier" |
| Qualify a claim | "acknowledge a constraint on the finding" |
| Draw a conclusion | "state a conclusion supported by the preceding discussion" |
| Signal a shift | "mark a transition from description to evaluation" |
| Provide context | "supply background necessary to understand the study" |

**Method:**

1. Read the sentence in question.
2. Read the sentence **before** and the sentence **after**. Function is defined
   by relationship, and the neighbors are where the relationship lives.
3. Describe the job in your own words: *"it's giving an example of the thing in
   the last sentence."*
4. Match to a choice.

**Pattern:** distractors are frequently accurate *descriptions of content*
rather than *statements of function*. If a choice tells you what the sentence
says instead of what it does, be suspicious.

**Pattern:** the credited answer often names a relationship (`supports`,
`qualifies`, `contrasts with`, `elaborates on`). Choices with no relational verb
are often content-restatements in disguise.

### Structure questions

You're describing the shape of the whole passage.

Common structures in short SAT passages:

- **Claim → evidence → qualification.** Very common in science passages.
- **Old view → new evidence → revised view.** Look for `long assumed`,
  `traditionally`, `until recently`.
- **Phenomenon → proposed explanation → assessment.**
- **General principle → specific case.**
- **Problem → attempted solution → remaining difficulty.**
- **Two views → comparison.**

**Method:** map the passage sentence by sentence in three or four words each
(*"states old view / gives new data / hedges"*). Then match.

**Pattern:** the credited answer accounts for the **entire** passage including
the last sentence. Distractors describe the first two-thirds accurately and stop
before the pivot. Always check the ending against the choice.

## Cross-Text Connections

**One or two per module.** Two short passages, and a question about their
relationship.

Often the hardest questions in the section, and worth budgeting 90-120 seconds.

### What the test asks

- "Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the
  [claim] in Text 1?"
- "Which choice best describes a difference in how the two texts discuss [X]?"
- "The author of Text 2 would most likely characterize the finding in Text 1
  as..."

### Method

1. **Read Text 1. Summarize its position in one sentence.** Write it down if
   you're on scratch paper.
2. **Read Text 2. Summarize its position in one sentence.**
3. **Name the relationship** before looking at choices:
   - Agreement, with Text 2 adding detail
   - Agreement about facts, disagreement about interpretation
   - Direct disagreement
   - Text 2 qualifies or limits Text 1
   - Text 2 offers an alternative explanation for the same observation
   - Text 2 addresses a different aspect entirely
4. Then evaluate choices against that relationship.

### Patterns

**Total disagreement is rarer than students expect.** The most common
relationship is **partial**: Text 2 accepts Text 1's data but disputes the
interpretation, or accepts the conclusion but limits its scope. Choices asserting
flat contradiction are frequently too strong.

**The answer is about the relationship, not either text alone.** A choice that
accurately describes Text 2's view but says nothing about Text 1 is usually
wrong on a relationship question.

**Watch for the shared object.** Both texts discuss something specific. The
question is almost always about *that* thing, not the surrounding material.

**"Would most likely respond" means using Text 2's stated principles.** Don't
invent a response. Find the principle Text 2 actually articulates and apply it
to Text 1's claim. The credited answer is usually a direct application, not a
creative extension.

### Traps

| Trap | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **One-text answer** | Describes Text 2 correctly, ignores the relationship |
| **Overstated conflict** | "Text 2 refutes Text 1" when Text 2 merely qualifies |
| **Invented agreement** | Claims consensus the texts don't establish |
| **Wrong axis** | Real difference between the texts, but not the one asked about |
| **Reversal** | Attributes Text 1's position to Text 2 |

The reversal trap is common and easy to fall for under time pressure. After
selecting, do a five-second check that you have the right author attached to the
right view.

## Drill plan for this domain

| Weakness | Filter | Volume and method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Words in Context | Words in Context, Easy → Hard | 40 items. **Cover the choices and write your prediction** for the first 20. |
| Function questions | Text Structure and Purpose, Medium | 20. For each, write the job in your own words before looking. |
| Structure questions | Text Structure and Purpose, Medium → Hard | 15. Map each passage sentence-by-sentence. |
| Cross-text | Cross-Text Connections, Medium → Hard | 20. Write both one-sentence summaries every time. |

The write-it-down instruction is doing real work in each row. These skills are
about *forcing articulation* before you're shown options. Once the habit is
automatic you can do it in your head, but not before.
