# SAT Reading and Writing — Standard English Conventions

**Catalog domain:** Standard English Conventions
**Skills:** Boundaries · Form, Structure, and Sense
**Roughly 26% of the section** — about 14 of 54 questions

This is the highest-leverage domain on the SAT. The rule set is **finite**, the
questions are **fast**, and the tested rules **repeat relentlessly**. A student
at 60% accuracy here can reach 90%+ in two to three weeks of focused work.

If your Reading and Writing score is below your target, check this domain first.

For the complete rule set with examples, see the
[grammar reference](05-grammar-reference.md). This file covers strategy and the
tested patterns.

---

## What these questions look like

- "Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of
  Standard English?"

That's essentially the only stem. Meaning, style, and elegance are irrelevant.
There is exactly one grammatically correct option; the other three are
grammatically wrong.

This is liberating. You are not making a judgment call. You are applying rules.

---

## The two skills

**Boundaries** — punctuation between and within sentences: periods, semicolons,
colons, commas, dashes; run-ons and fragments; joining clauses.

**Form, Structure, and Sense** — verbs, pronouns, agreement, modifiers,
parallelism, possessives, comparisons.

Boundaries is the larger and more mechanical of the two. Start there.

---

## Boundaries: the master method

Almost every punctuation question on the SAT resolves with one procedure.

### Step 1 — Classify both sides

For the punctuation in question, determine:

- Is the text **before** it an independent clause? (subject + verb, could stand
  alone)
- Is the text **after** it an independent clause?

### Step 2 — Apply the join table

| Before | After | Legal | Illegal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Independent** | **Independent** | Period · Semicolon · Comma + FANBOYS · Colon (if second explains first) · Dash | Comma alone (comma splice) · No punctuation (run-on) |
| **Independent** | **Dependent / phrase** | Comma · Colon (if it introduces) · Dash · Nothing | Semicolon · Period |
| **Dependent** | **Independent** | Comma | Semicolon · Period |
| **Phrase** | **Phrase** | Usually nothing | Most punctuation |

That table answers the large majority of Boundaries questions. Memorize it.

### Step 3 — Check the special cases

- **Semicolon = period.** Anywhere a semicolon is legal, a period is legal, and
  vice versa. If two choices offer a semicolon and a period doing the same job,
  **both are wrong** — they can't both be credited. This is a free double
  elimination and it appears often.
- **Colon needs a complete sentence before it.** What follows can be a list, a
  phrase, or a clause. What precedes must be independent.
- **Dashes work like commas or colons**, but must be used **in pairs** when
  setting off an interruption. One dash cannot pair with one comma.
- **Conjunctive adverbs are not conjunctions.** `However`, `therefore`,
  `moreover`, `nevertheless`, `consequently`, `furthermore`, `thus`,
  `otherwise`, `instead` cannot join two independent clauses with just a comma.
  `X, however, Y` is a comma splice. You need `X; however, Y` or `X. However,
  Y`.

That last rule is tested constantly and missed constantly.

### The FANBOYS list

Coordinating conjunctions: **F**or, **A**nd, **N**or, **B**ut, **O**r, **Y**et,
**S**o. Only these seven can join two independent clauses with a comma.

## Comma rules the SAT actually tests

Most students over-use commas because they punctuate by ear — inserting a comma
wherever they'd pause. The SAT is not testing pauses.

### Commas that are required

| Situation | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Before FANBOYS joining two independent clauses | *The data were clear**,** but the conclusion was disputed.* |
| After an introductory element | *After reviewing the results**,** the team revised its estimate.* |
| Around non-essential information (both sides) | *The study**,** published in 2019**,** was widely cited.* |
| Between items in a list | *soil**,** water**,** and light* |
| After a dependent clause that starts a sentence | *Because funding lapsed**,** the survey ended.* |

### Commas that are forbidden

These are the tested errors. Each one kills a choice instantly.

| Never | Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Between subject and verb | *The researchers who ran the trial**,** published...* | *...trial published...* |
| Between verb and object | *She discovered**,** a new species.* | *She discovered a new species.* |
| Before a dependent clause at the end (usually) | *He left**,** because it rained.* | *He left because it rained.* |
| Around essential information | *The book**,** that I ordered**,** arrived.* | *The book that I ordered arrived.* |
| Between two verbs sharing a subject | *She wrote the report**,** and submitted it.* | *She wrote the report and submitted it.* |
| Comma splice | *It rained**,** we stayed inside.* | *It rained, so we stayed inside.* |

**The subject-verb comma is the single most common wrong-answer construction in
this domain.** It's attractive because long subjects create a natural pause. Get
in the habit of identifying the main subject and main verb and checking that
nothing separates them.

### Essential vs. non-essential

The distinction that drives most comma questions.

- **Non-essential** — removable without changing which thing you mean. Takes
  commas (or dashes, or parentheses) on **both** sides.
- **Essential** — identifies which one. Takes **no** commas.

> *My brother**,** who lives in Chicago**,** is a nurse.*
> (I have one brother. The clause is extra information.)
>
> *My brother who lives in Chicago is a nurse.*
> (I have several brothers. The clause specifies which one.)

**The removal test:** take out the material between commas. If the sentence
still makes sense and means the same thing, the commas are correct. If the
sentence becomes vague or ungrammatical, the material is essential and the
commas are wrong.

**One-sided punctuation is always wrong.** If a choice puts a comma before an
interruption but not after, eliminate it without reading further. This is an
extremely fast elimination.

## Form, Structure, and Sense

### Subject-verb agreement

The test's method is always the same: **separate the subject from the verb** so
you can't hear the agreement.

> *The **collection** of rare manuscripts donated by several alumni **is**
> housed in the east wing.*

Subject is *collection* (singular). Everything between is a prepositional phrase.
Cross those out and the agreement is obvious.

**Constructions the SAT uses to hide the subject:**

| Construction | Example | Subject |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prepositional phrase | *the box of nails* | box |
| Relative clause | *the students who took the exam* | students |
| Appositive | *Marie Curie, the physicist,* | Marie Curie |
| Inverted order | *Among the findings **were** three surprises* | three surprises |
| "There is/are" | *There **are** several options* | several options |

**Rules to know:**

- Phrases like *along with*, *as well as*, *in addition to*, *together with*,
  *including* do **not** make a singular subject plural.
  *The manager, along with two assistants, **was** present.*
- **Either/or, neither/nor:** the verb agrees with the **nearer** subject.
- Collective nouns (*team, committee, group, jury*) are normally singular.
- *Each, every, either, neither, one, none, everyone, someone, nobody* →
  singular.
- Gerund phrases as subjects → singular. *Running marathons **is** demanding.*

### Verb tense

Keep tense consistent unless the passage signals a shift. Look for time markers:
*in 1897*, *currently*, *by the time*, *since*, *until recently*, *had
previously*.

**Past perfect (*had* + past participle)** is for an action completed **before**
another past action. The SAT tests this when two past events are sequenced.

> *By the time the funding arrived, the team **had disbanded**.*

**Present perfect (*has/have* + past participle)** is for an action starting in
the past and continuing, or completed at an unspecified time.

### Pronouns

**Agreement:** a pronoun matches its antecedent in number.

- *Each* student brought **his or her** book (not *their*, on the SAT).
- A *company*, *team*, or *organization* is **it**, not *they*.

**Clarity:** if a pronoun could refer to two things, it's ambiguous, and the SAT
treats ambiguity as an error.

**Case:**

| Subject | Object | Possessive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I, he, she, we, they, who | me, him, her, us, them, whom | my, his, her, our, their, whose |

For *who* vs. *whom*: substitute *he/him*. If *he* works, use *who*. If *him*
works, use *whom*.

**The apostrophe set** — tested almost every form:

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **its** | possessive |
| **it's** | it is / it has |
| **their** | possessive |
| **they're** | they are |
| **there** | location |
| **whose** | possessive |
| **who's** | who is / who has |
| **your** / **you're** | possessive / you are |

### Possessives

| Form | Rule | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Singular | add `'s` | the *student's* essay |
| Plural ending in s | add `'` | the *students'* essays |
| Plural not ending in s | add `'s` | the *children's* books |
| Singular ending in s | add `'s` (SAT convention) | *Dickens's* novels |

**The plural-vs-possessive question** appears constantly and looks like this:
four choices offering *researchers*, *researcher's*, *researchers'*,
*researchers's*. Decide two things: how many researchers, and is anything being
possessed?

### Modifiers

A modifying phrase at the start of a sentence must modify the **subject that
immediately follows**.

> ✗ *Having studied for weeks, the exam felt manageable.*
> (The exam didn't study.)
>
> ✓ *Having studied for weeks, she found the exam manageable.*

**Method:** when a sentence opens with a participial phrase (*-ing* or *-ed*) or
a descriptive phrase, read the phrase, then look at the very next noun. Ask "did
that noun do this?" If not, eliminate.

This is a fast, mechanical check and it usually eliminates two choices at once.

### Parallelism

Items in a list or comparison must share grammatical form.

> ✗ *hiking, swimming, and **to bike***
> ✓ *hiking, swimming, and **biking***

Also applies to correlative pairs — the structure after each half must match:

*not only ... but also* · *either ... or* · *neither ... nor* ·
*both ... and* · *not ... but*

### Comparisons

Compare like to like.

> ✗ *The **climate** of Peru is milder than **Chile**.*
> (Comparing a climate to a country.)
>
> ✓ *The climate of Peru is milder than **that of** Chile.*

Watch for `that of`, `those of`, `unlike`, `compared to`. Also:

- **Fewer** (countable) vs. **less** (uncountable)
- **Number** (countable) vs. **amount** (uncountable)
- **Between** (two) vs. **among** (three or more)
- Comparative (*-er*, *more*) for two; superlative (*-est*, *most*) for three+

## Strategy for this domain

### Vertical scanning

Before reading the full sentence, look **down the four choices** and note what
varies. The difference tells you which rule is being tested.

| What varies | Rule being tested |
| --- | --- |
| Commas, semicolons, periods | Boundaries |
| `is` / `are` / `was` / `were` | Subject-verb agreement |
| `its` / `it's` | Apostrophe |
| `researcher's` / `researchers'` | Possessive |
| `who` / `whom` | Case |
| Verb forms across tenses | Tense consistency |
| Word order in the phrase | Modifier placement |

This takes three seconds and focuses your reading. It is the single best speed
technique in this domain.

### The elimination cascade

1. **Semicolon and period both present, doing the same job?** Eliminate both.
2. **One-sided punctuation** around an interruption? Eliminate.
3. **Comma between subject and verb?** Eliminate.
4. **Comma splice?** Eliminate.
5. Apply the specific rule to what remains.

Steps 1-4 frequently leave you with one choice before you've thought about the
sentence's meaning at all.

### Do not punctuate by ear

Your ear was trained on speech, which pauses in places where written English
takes no punctuation. If your reasoning is "it sounds like there should be a
comma there," you are guessing.

Replace ear with procedure: identify clauses, apply the join table.

## Drill plan

This domain rewards volume more than any other. The rules are few and they
repeat.

| Stage | Filter | Volume |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Boundaries mechanics | Boundaries, Easy | 25 untimed. Write "IND" or "DEP" for each side of the punctuation. |
| 2. Boundaries under time | Boundaries, Medium → Hard | 40 timed at 35 sec each |
| 3. Agreement and pronouns | Form, Structure, and Sense, Easy → Medium | 30. Cross out the phrases between subject and verb. |
| 4. Modifiers and parallelism | Form, Structure, and Sense, Medium → Hard | 25 |
| 5. Mixed | Both skills, all difficulties | 50 timed at 35 sec each |

**Target: 90%+ accuracy at 35 seconds per question.** That is achievable and it
banks four to five minutes per module for the reading questions.

If you're missing these, the fix is almost always one of three things: you're
punctuating by ear, you're not identifying the subject, or you don't know the
join table. All three are fixed by the same drilling.
