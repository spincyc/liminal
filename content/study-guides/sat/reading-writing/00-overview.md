# SAT Reading and Writing — Overview

**54 questions · 64 minutes · two 27-question modules of 32 minutes each**

Half your total SAT score. Despite the name, it is one integrated section: short
passages, one question each, covering reading comprehension, rhetoric, and
grammar.

---

## What makes this section distinctive

**Every question has its own passage.** Typically 25-150 words. There are no
long shared passages except for the occasional paired texts. This is the biggest
departure from older formats and from the ACT.

Consequences:

- **You reset constantly.** 27 unrelated topics per module. Mental agility
  matters more than reading endurance.
- **You cannot get lost.** Even if a passage's topic is unfamiliar, you only
  need to understand 100 words.
- **Background knowledge is nearly useless**, and occasionally harmful. Every
  answer must come from the text in front of you.
- **Reading speed matters less than usual.** Careful reading of a short passage
  is affordable. Read it properly.

---

## The order of questions

Questions appear in a fixed sequence by domain, and roughly easy-to-hard within
each domain. Approximate shape of a module:

| Position | Domain | Roughly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Craft and Structure — Words in Context | ~6-8 questions |
| 2 | Craft and Structure — Text Structure and Purpose, Cross-Text | ~2-4 |
| 3 | Information and Ideas — Central Ideas, Evidence, Inferences | ~7-9 |
| 4 | Standard English Conventions — Boundaries, Form/Structure/Sense | ~6-8 |
| 5 | Expression of Ideas — Transitions, Rhetorical Synthesis | ~4-6 |

Exact counts vary by form. What's reliable is the **order**, and it's useful:

- You know grammar is coming in the back half.
- You know the final questions are Rhetorical Synthesis (the bullet-point ones).
- If you're behind pace, you know what you're about to sacrifice.

### Domain weighting

| Domain | Approximate share of section |
| --- | ---: |
| Information and Ideas | 26% |
| Craft and Structure | 28% |
| Standard English Conventions | 26% |
| Expression of Ideas | 20% |

Roughly a quarter of your Reading and Writing score is **pure grammar** — a
finite, learnable rule set. For most students this is the fastest available
improvement in the entire SAT. See
[Standard English Conventions](04-standard-english-conventions.md) and the

## Pacing

71 seconds per question on average. But the distribution should be uneven:

| Question type | Target time |
| --- | --- |
| Standard English Conventions | 30-45 sec |
| Words in Context | 45-60 sec |
| Transitions | 45-60 sec |
| Central Ideas, Inferences | 60-90 sec |
| Command of Evidence (quantitative) | 75-100 sec |
| Cross-Text Connections | 90-120 sec |
| Rhetorical Synthesis | 60-90 sec |

Banking 30 seconds on each of eight grammar questions buys you four minutes for
the hard inference and paired-passage items.

**Checkpoints:** Q7 by 8 min, Q14 by 16 min, Q21 by 24 min, done by 30 min.

**The conventions-first option:** since you can navigate freely within a module,
some students jump to the grammar block first to bank time, then work the
reading questions. Worth testing in practice. It helps students who run out of
time and disorients students who don't.

## The universal method

For every question in this section:

1. **Read the question stem first.** Fifteen words, and it tells you what to
   look for. Reading the passage without knowing the task means reading it
   twice.
2. **Read the whole passage.** It's short. Do not skim — the credited answer
   frequently hinges on one qualifying word.
3. **Predict before looking.** Answer in your own words first. This is the
   single most protective habit in the section: a prediction makes you immune to
   attractive distractors.
4. **Eliminate against your prediction.** Cross off, with a reason.
5. **Verify with the text.** Point to the words that support your choice. If you
   can't, reconsider.

Step 3 is the one people skip. It costs five seconds and prevents the most
common failure in the section, which is being talked into a wrong answer by a
well-written distractor.

## What the wrong answers look like here

Recurring distractor shapes across all reading question types:

| Shape | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **True but irrelevant** | Accurate about the passage, doesn't answer this question |
| **Too extreme** | Right direction, overstated (`proves`, `all`, `never`) |
| **Too broad / too narrow** | Wrong scope for what was asked |
| **Half-right** | First clause correct, second clause false |
| **Outside knowledge** | True in the world, unsupported by the text |
| **Word-match trap** | Repeats passage vocabulary, distorts the meaning |
| **Opposite** | Reverses the relationship the passage describes |

The word-match trap deserves special attention on this section. Because passages
are short, students under time pressure look for vocabulary overlap. Item
writers know this and build distractors from passage words arranged into false
claims.

## Reading the stimulus efficiently

For each passage, get three things:

1. **Topic** — what is this about?
2. **Claim** — what is the author asserting?
3. **Structure** — is there a pivot? Look for `but`, `however`, `although`,
   `yet`, `nevertheless`, `in contrast`, `on the other hand`.

That third one is disproportionately useful. In a 100-word passage, the pivot
word is usually where the tested idea lives. When a short passage contains a
`however`, the point is almost always after it.

### Passage types you'll see

| Type | What to watch for |
| --- | --- |
| Literature excerpt | Tone and character attitude; don't over-infer emotion |
| Science / research summary | The finding, and its limits; hedging is meaningful |
| Social science with data | Match the claim to the actual data direction |
| History / founding documents | Argument structure and the author's position |
| Humanities / arts criticism | The critic's stance versus the described work |
| Paired texts | The *relationship* between the two |

## Scoring realities

- Roughly 4 of the 54 questions are unscored pretest items.
- Module 1 determines your Module 2 routing. Don't warm up through it.
- Within a module you can skip, flag, and return freely.
- Grammar questions are the cheapest points available. If your conventions
  accuracy is below 85%, fix that before anything else in this section.
