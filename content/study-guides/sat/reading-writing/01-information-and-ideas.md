# SAT Reading and Writing — Information and Ideas

**Catalog domain:** Information and Ideas
**Skills:** Central Ideas and Details · Inferences · Command of Evidence
**Roughly 26% of the section** — about 14 of 54 questions

This domain tests whether you can extract what a text says, what it implies, and
what evidence supports a claim. It contains the section's hardest questions and
its most rewarding ones to train.

---

## Central Ideas and Details

### What the test asks

- "Which choice best states the main idea of the text?"
- "According to the text, what is true about [X]?"
- "The text most strongly suggests that..." *(borderline inference)*
- "Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?"

Two distinct question types hide under this skill: **global** (main idea) and
**local** (specific detail). Identify which you have before reading.

### Method — main idea

1. Read the passage and find the **claim sentence**. In a short SAT passage it's
   usually the first or last sentence, or the sentence right after a pivot word.
2. State the main idea in your own words in under ten words.
3. Eliminate choices that are about a **detail** rather than the whole.
4. Eliminate choices that are **broader than the passage** — describing a whole
   field when the passage described one study.
5. Check remaining choices for **half-right** structure.

### Method — detail

1. Note exactly what the stem asks for.
2. Locate the relevant sentence.
3. Read that sentence **and the one after it**. Qualifications frequently live
   in the following sentence.
4. Match literally. On detail questions, close textual correspondence is
   correct — this is the one place where a near-verbatim answer is a good sign.

### Patterns

**Main-idea answers are the flattest option.** They summarize without adding
interpretation. If a choice is more interesting than the passage, it's wrong.

**Main-idea answers cover the whole passage.** Run this test: does this choice
account for the *last* sentence? Distractors often accurately describe the first
half and ignore a pivot.

**Detail answers must be stated, not implied.** "According to the text" means
the text says it. A reasonable-sounding extension is the wrong answer.

**Watch the qualifier.** If the passage says a treatment worked "in mice," a
choice about "patients" is wrong. If it says "in preliminary trials," a choice
stating an established result is wrong. Short passages are dense with
qualifiers, and they are load-bearing.

### Traps

| Trap | Example shape |
| --- | --- |
| **Detail as main idea** | Picks a real supporting fact, presents it as the point |
| **Ignoring the pivot** | Summarizes the setup, misses the `however` |
| **Scope inflation** | Passage describes one experiment; choice describes the field |
| **Certainty inflation** | Passage hedges; choice states flatly |
| **Wrong subject** | Passage is about the *method*; choice is about the *result* |

### Worked reasoning

> *Astronomers have long assumed that the rings of Saturn formed alongside the
> planet itself, roughly 4.5 billion years ago. However, measurements from the
> Cassini probe indicate that the rings' ice remains unusually bright and free
> of accumulated dust, a condition that would be difficult to maintain over such
> a span. Some researchers now argue the rings may be only 100 million years
> old, though the estimate depends on assumptions about dust infall rates that
> remain contested.*
>
> **Which choice best states the main idea of the text?**

*Prediction:* new evidence challenges the old age estimate, but the new estimate
isn't settled.

Now evaluate answer shapes:

- A choice saying **"the rings formed with Saturn 4.5 billion years ago"** —
  this is the view being *challenged*. Ignoring the pivot.
- A choice saying **"Cassini measurements prove the rings are 100 million years
  old"** — two problems: `prove` is certainty inflation, and the passage
  explicitly says the estimate depends on contested assumptions.
- A choice saying **"dust infall rates are poorly understood"** — true per the
  passage, but it's the final qualifier, not the point. Detail as main idea.
- A choice saying **"evidence has led some researchers to propose a much younger
  age for the rings, though the conclusion remains uncertain"** — flat, covers
  the pivot, preserves the hedge. **This is the shape of a credited answer.**

Notice the credited answer is the least interesting one. That's the pattern.

## Inferences

The most-missed skill in the section.

### What the test asks

- "Which choice most logically completes the text?"
- "Based on the text, it can most reasonably be inferred that..."
- "The text most strongly suggests..."

The "most logically completes the text" form ends the passage with a blank and
asks you to finish it. It is an inference question wearing a different hat.

### The standard: *must*, not *could*

This is the whole skill.

> The correct answer is the choice that **must** be true given the text — not
> the one that could be true, not the one that's likely, not the one a
> reasonable person would guess.

The SAT's inferences are **small**. One step. Often less than you expect. If a
choice requires you to add any information not in the passage, it's wrong, no
matter how sensible.

### Method

1. Read the passage and identify the **logical structure**. What's given? What
   direction is the argument going?
2. For "most logically completes," look at the sentence *before* the blank and
   its connector. `Therefore` means the blank is a conclusion. `However` means
   it's a contrast. `Because` means it's a cause.
3. **Predict.** Say what must follow.
4. Test each choice: *"Could every sentence in this passage be true while this
   choice is false?"* If yes → eliminate.

That test is mechanical and it works. Use it explicitly on hard items.

### Patterns

**The credited inference is unexciting.** It usually restates a relationship
already implied, one small step on.

**Hedged language wins.** `Some`, `may`, `at least`, `not necessarily`. An
absolute inference is nearly always over-reaching.

**Watch the connector before the blank.** It constrains the answer more than the
passage content does. Half of these questions are resolvable from the connector
plus one sentence.

**Negative and limiting inferences are common.** Answers of the form "X is not
sufficient to explain Y" or "the relationship is not as simple as previously
assumed" appear often, because they're the safest thing a text can support.

### Traps

| Trap | Why it's attractive |
| --- | --- |
| **Plausible extension** | Sensible, real-world true, unsupported |
| **Causal leap** | Passage shows correlation; choice asserts cause |
| **Reversal** | Correct relationship, backwards direction |
| **Over-generalization** | One case → universal claim |
| **Right idea, wrong strength** | The passage's point, stated too absolutely |

The **causal leap** is worth memorizing. If a passage reports that two things
occur together, an inference that one causes the other is wrong unless the text
says so.

### Worked reasoning

> *A researcher compared two groups of office workers: one using
> height-adjustable desks and one using fixed desks. After six months, the
> adjustable-desk group reported less back discomfort. However, participants
> chose their own group assignment, and those who selected adjustable desks were
> more likely to report regular exercise habits at the study's outset. The
> researcher therefore concluded that ______*

*Prediction:* the connector is `therefore`, and the preceding sentence is a
methodological objection — self-selection plus a confounding variable. So the
conclusion must be a *limitation*.

- **"adjustable desks reduce back discomfort"** — the causal leap. The
  paragraph's second half exists specifically to block this.
- **"exercise is more effective than adjustable desks for back pain"** — the
  study didn't compare those. Unsupported.
- **"the study's design does not permit a firm conclusion about the desks'
  effect"** — follows directly from the stated confound. **Credited shape.**
- **"office workers should be assigned to desk types at random"** — a
  recommendation, not an inference from this text. Plausible extension.

## Command of Evidence

Two flavors: **textual** and **quantitative**.

### Textual Command of Evidence

**Stem:** "Which quotation from [text] most effectively illustrates the claim?"

You're given a claim and four quotations. Pick the one that demonstrates *that
specific claim*.

**Method:**

1. **Restate the claim precisely**, including its qualifiers. This is where
   these are won or lost — the claim usually has two parts.
2. For each quotation, ask: does this show **exactly this**?
3. Eliminate quotations that are on-topic but demonstrate something adjacent.

**Patterns:**

- Distractors are usually **on-topic and clearly relevant** — that's the trap.
  Relevance is not sufficiency.
- If the claim is about a *contrast*, the quotation must contain both sides.
- If the claim is about a *change over time*, the quotation must show both
  before and after.
- If the claim is about the author's *attitude*, the quotation must contain
  evaluative language, not neutral description.

**The two-part claim rule:** most claims in these items have two components
(e.g., "the character's outward calm conceals private anxiety"). The credited
quotation contains **both**. Distractors contain one. Underline both parts
before reading the quotations.

### Quantitative Command of Evidence

**Stem:** "Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to support the
claim?" or "...to complete the text?"

A short passage plus a table, bar chart, line graph, or scatterplot.

**Method:**

1. **Read the graph's title, axes, units, and legend before anything else.**
   Ten seconds. This prevents the majority of errors here.
2. Identify what the passage's claim needs the data to show.
3. For each choice, verify **two things independently**:
   - Is this statement **accurate** as a reading of the data?
   - Does it **support the specific claim**?
4. Eliminate anything that fails either test.

**Patterns:**

Distractors here come in exactly three flavors, and naming them is most of the
skill:

| Flavor | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Accurate but unsupportive** | Correctly reads the data; doesn't bear on the claim |
| **Inaccurate but supportive** | Would support the claim if true; misreads the data |
| **Both wrong** | Misreads the data and misses the claim |

You must check both dimensions. Students who check only accuracy pick the first
flavor; students who check only relevance pick the second.

**Specific things that go wrong:**

- Reading the wrong series in a multi-line graph
- Missing a second y-axis on the right
- Confusing "greatest value" with "greatest increase"
- Missing a unit prefix (thousands, millions) in the axis label
- Reversing which group is which in the legend
- Reading a cumulative graph as if it showed per-period values

**Comparison language precision:**

| Phrase | Means |
| --- | --- |
| "greater than" | Absolute comparison at one point |
| "increased more" | Change comparison, absolute |
| "increased by a greater percentage" | Change comparison, relative — different answer |
| "consistently higher" | True at *every* point, not on average |

That last one kills a lot of choices. "Consistently" is an absolute — one
crossing point makes it false.

## Drill plan for this domain

| Weakness | Filter | Volume |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main idea | Central Ideas and Details, Medium | 20 untimed, then 20 timed |
| Detail accuracy | Central Ideas and Details, Easy → Medium | 25 |
| Inference | Inferences, Medium → Hard | 30, with the must/could test written out |
| Textual evidence | Command of Evidence, Medium | 20, underlining both claim parts |
| Data | Command of Evidence, Medium → Hard | 25, narrating axes aloud first |

For inference specifically: on your first 15 items, **write down** why each
eliminated choice could be false while the passage stays true. It's slow. It
builds the reflex, and the reflex is the entire skill.
