# SAT Math — Overview

**44 questions · 70 minutes · two 22-question modules of 35 minutes each**

Half your total SAT score. Calculator permitted throughout, with the Desmos
graphing calculator built into Bluebook.

---

## Structure

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Format | ~75% multiple choice (4 options), ~25% student-produced response |
| Calculator | Permitted on every question; Desmos built in |
| Reference sheet | Provided on screen (short — see [formula reference](05-formula-reference.md)) |
| Ordering | Roughly easy → hard within each module |
| Adaptivity | Module 2 difficulty depends on Module 1 performance |

About 4 of the 44 questions are unscored pretest items.

time, that's where it goes. Geometry is worth roughly six questions — real, but
not where a struggling student should start.

## Pacing

95 seconds per question on average, but spend unevenly:

| Question position | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Q1-8 (easy) | 45-70 sec |
| Q9-16 (medium) | 90-110 sec |
| Q17-22 (hard) | 120-150 sec |

**Checkpoints:** Q6 by 9 min, Q12 by 18 min, Q18 by 26 min, done by 33 min.

Because questions run easy to hard, **the correct thing to sacrifice is the
end**, not the middle. If you're behind, don't rush questions 10-15 to reach
question 22. Those middle questions are far more gettable.

## Grid-in rules

Memorize these. A formatting error loses a point you earned.

- **Up to 5 characters** (6 with a negative sign)
- **No** commas, dollar signs, percent signs, or units
- **Fractions and decimals both accepted**; improper fractions are fine
- **Mixed numbers are not accepted** — `3 1/2` reads as `31/2`. Enter `7/2` or
  `3.5`
- **Repeating decimals must fill the grid**: `2/3` → `.6666` or `.6667`, not
  `.67`
- **Negatives are allowed**
- If multiple answers are valid, enter **one**
- Don't round unless asked; if you must, fill the available space

**Grid-ins have no answer choices**, which removes backsolving and removes the
safety net of noticing your answer isn't listed. Check these more carefully.

## The four techniques that carry the section

Before any domain-specific content, these four general methods solve or
accelerate a large fraction of SAT Math questions.

### 1. Plugging in numbers (for variable answer choices)

When the question and the choices both contain variables, pick concrete values.

**Rules:** avoid `0` and `1` (they make too many expressions equal). Avoid
numbers already in the problem. Use small, easy values. If percentages are
involved, use `100`.

> *If a store increases a price by 20% and then discounts the new price by 20%,
> the final price is what percent of the original?*
>
> Let the original be **100**. After +20%: **120**. After −20% of 120: 120 − 24
> = **96**. Answer: **96%**.

No algebra required. This is faster and less error-prone than symbolic
manipulation.

**When more than one choice survives**, try a second set of numbers. Only test
the survivors.

### 2. Backsolving (for numeric answer choices)

When the choices are plain numbers and setting up the algebra is ugly, plug the
choices into the problem.

**Start with the second-smallest or second-largest.** If choices are ordered and
yours is too big, you know the direction — you rarely test more than two.

### 3. Estimation

Before computing, get a rough sense of the answer's size and sign. This
eliminates choices and catches errors.

If a rate problem's answer should be "a bit less than 50" and the choices are
`12`, `47`, `188`, `540`, you're done.

### 4. Desmos

Graph it. See the [Desmos playbook](06-desmos-playbook.md).

Anything of the form "where do these meet," "what are the zeros," "for what
value of k does this have one solution," "what is the vertex," "which system has
no solution" is faster graphed than solved. Desmos also does regressions,
statistics, and inequality shading.

## What the wrong answers are made of

SAT Math distractors are constructed from specific, predictable errors:

| Distractor source | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **The intermediate value** | You solve for `x`; they ask for `2x`. The value of `x` is a choice. |
| **Sign error** | Both `7` and `−7` present |
| **Reciprocal** | `3/4` vs `4/3` |
| **Off by one** | Counting endpoints incorrectly |
| **Wrong operation** | Added when the problem needed subtraction |
| **Unit not converted** | The answer in minutes when hours were asked |
| **Percent confusion** | Percent *of* vs. percent *change* vs. percent *remaining* |
| **Diameter for radius** | Or vice versa |
| **Forgot a step** | The answer to the second-to-last step |

**Because of this, your answer appearing among the choices means nothing.** It
is the expected result of making a standard mistake. Always verify
independently: substitute back, estimate, or check units.

## The three habits that fix most math errors

For most students, math score is limited by mechanics rather than knowledge.
These three habits are worth more than any content review:

**1. Circle what the question asks.** Before solving. The single most common
error on this section is solving correctly and answering the wrong question.

**2. Write every step.** No mental arithmetic beyond one operation. Sign errors
and dropped terms come from doing two steps at once. This costs seconds and
saves points.

**3. Substitute your answer back.** For equations, this is nearly free and it
catches almost everything.

If your error log is dominated by careless errors — and for most students above
a mid score it is — these three habits *are* your study plan for the next two
weeks. See [Error log and review method](../../07-error-log-and-review-method.md).
