# ACT English — Overview

**50 questions · 35 minutes · 42 seconds per question**

One of the three sections in your Composite. Five passages, each with about ten
questions, most tied to an underlined portion of the text.

For most students this is the **easiest section to improve** on the entire ACT.
The rule set is finite, the questions are fast, and pure grammar is roughly half
the section.

---

## Structure

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Passages | 5, each ~10 questions |
| Question style | Underlined portion + 4 choices, first usually "NO CHANGE" |
| Also | Boxed questions about a paragraph or the whole passage |
| Pacing | 7 minutes per passage |

### Domain weighting

| Domain | Share | Approx. questions |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| [Production of Writing](01-production-of-writing.md) | ~41% | ~20 |
| [Knowledge of Language](02-knowledge-of-language.md) | ~21% | ~11 |
| [Conventions of Standard English](03-conventions-of-standard-english.md) | ~38% | ~19 |

Roughly **half the section is mechanical**: punctuation, grammar, usage, and
concision. Those questions take 15-25 seconds each once you know the rules,
which buys the time you need for the rhetorical questions.

---

## The two question formats

### Underlined-portion questions

Most of the section. A phrase is underlined; you pick the best version.

- **"NO CHANGE" is a real answer** and is correct roughly a quarter of the time.
  Do not avoid it out of suspicion.
- Choices frequently differ by a single punctuation mark. Read them
  character by character.

### Boxed questions

Marked with a numbered box in the text or asked at the end. These ask about
purpose, placement, addition, or deletion:

- "The writer is considering deleting the preceding sentence. Should the
  sentence be kept or deleted?"
- "Which choice most effectively introduces the paragraph?"
- "Suppose the writer's goal had been to [X]. Would this essay accomplish that
  goal?"
- "For the sake of logic and coherence, Paragraph 3 should be placed..."

These take longer and require reading surrounding context. Budget for them.

---

## How to read the passage

**Do not read the whole passage first.** You don't have time and you don't need
to.

**Standard approach:**

1. Read from the **beginning of the sentence** containing the underlined
   portion.
2. Read **through the end of that sentence** — including past the underline.
3. For punctuation and transition questions, also read the **previous
   sentence**.
4. For boxed questions about a paragraph, read the **whole paragraph**.
5. For whole-essay questions (usually the last question), you need a general
   sense of the passage — which you'll have accumulated by then.

**Never** evaluate an underlined portion by reading only the underlined words.
Agreement, punctuation, and modifier errors are all defined by what surrounds
them.

## Pacing

7 minutes per passage. Checkpoints:

| At | Finished |
| --- | --- |
| 7 min | Passage 1 (Q10) |
| 14 min | Passage 2 (Q20) |
| 21 min | Passage 3 (Q30) |
| 28 min | Passage 4 (Q40) |
| 35 min | Passage 5 (Q50) |

Target finishing with 2-3 minutes to spare. Achievable, and it's where
struggling ACT students find their biggest gains.

**Speed comes from the mechanical questions.** If grammar questions take you 45
seconds each, you have no time for the rhetorical ones. Getting punctuation to
20 seconds is the whole game.

## The three governing principles

The ACT has an aesthetic. Internalize it and the section gets much easier.

### 1. Shorter is better

When two choices are grammatically correct and mean the same thing, **the
shorter one is credited.** Wordiness is treated as an error, not a style
preference.

Corollary: **"DELETE the underlined portion" / "OMIT"** is correct noticeably
more often than 25% of the time. Consider it seriously every time it appears.

*Fails when:* the shorter version loses needed information, breaks grammar, or
creates ambiguity.

### 2. Clearer is better

Ambiguous pronouns, misplaced modifiers, and awkward constructions are errors
even when technically grammatical.

### 3. Relevance rules

Material that doesn't serve the paragraph's point should go, however
interesting. On "should the writer add this sentence" questions, the answer is
frequently **no**, because the addition is off-topic.

## Vertical scanning — the core speed technique

Before reading the sentence, **look down the four choices** and note what
varies. That tells you what's being tested.

| What varies across choices | Being tested |
| --- | --- |
| Commas, semicolons, periods, dashes | Punctuation / boundaries |
| `is`/`are`, `was`/`were` | Subject-verb agreement |
| `its`/`it's`, `their`/`they're`/`there` | Apostrophes |
| `runner's`/`runners'`/`runners` | Possessive vs. plural |
| `who`/`whom` | Pronoun case |
| Different verb tenses | Tense consistency |
| Length — one short, three long | Concision |
| Completely different wording | Word choice or rhetorical purpose |
| Transitions (`however`, `therefore`...) | Logical relationship |

Three seconds of scanning focuses everything that follows. This is the highest-
value habit in the section.

---

## The elimination cascade

Run this before thinking hard:

1. **Semicolon and period both offered, doing the same job?** Both wrong — they
   are grammatically identical, so neither can be uniquely correct.
2. **One-sided punctuation** around an interrupter? Wrong.
3. **Comma between subject and verb?** Wrong.
4. **Comma splice?** Wrong.
5. **Two choices that mean exactly the same thing?** Both wrong.
6. Of what's left, prefer the **shortest** correct option.

Steps 1-5 frequently leave one choice standing before you've considered meaning.

---

## What separates a 26 from a 33 here

Not knowledge — nearly all high scorers know the rules. The difference is:

- **Speed on mechanical questions**, freeing time for rhetorical ones
- **Not second-guessing NO CHANGE**
- **Reading the full sentence** rather than the underlined fragment
- **Answering the stated goal** on rhetorical questions rather than picking the
  nicest sentence
- **Not punctuating by ear**

Every one of those is a habit, trainable in two to three weeks.
