# ACT English — Conventions of Standard English

**Catalog domain:** Conventions of Standard English
**Skills:** Sentence Structure and Formation · Usage · Punctuation
**~38% of the section** — about 19 questions

Pure grammar. Finite rules, fast questions, and the fastest available
improvement on ACT English.

Target: **20-25 seconds per question at 90%+ accuracy.** That banks the time you
need for [Production of Writing](01-production-of-writing.md).

The complete rule set with examples is in the
[grammar reference](../../sat/reading-writing/05-grammar-reference.md). This file
covers ACT-specific strategy and the highest-frequency patterns.

## Punctuation

The largest and most mechanical part of this domain. The ACT tests punctuation
more heavily than the SAT does.

### The master method

For any punctuation question, classify both sides:

1. Is what comes **before** an independent clause?
2. Is what comes **after** an independent clause?

Then apply:

| Before | After | Legal | Illegal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Independent** | **Independent** | Period · Semicolon · Comma + FANBOYS · Colon (if explanatory) · Dash | Comma alone · Nothing |
| **Independent** | **Dependent/phrase** | Comma · Colon · Dash · Nothing | Semicolon · Period |
| **Dependent** | **Independent** | Comma | Semicolon · Period |
| **Phrase** | **Phrase** | Usually nothing | Most punctuation |

This resolves the majority of ACT punctuation questions mechanically, with no
judgment.

### The free eliminations

Run these before thinking:

**1. Semicolon and period doing the same job → both wrong.**
They are grammatically identical. Two identical answers can't both be credited.

**2. One-sided punctuation → wrong.**
An interrupter needs matching punctuation on both sides. A comma before and
nothing after, or a dash before and a comma after, is always wrong.

**3. Comma between subject and verb → wrong.**
Always. Long subjects create a natural pause and the ACT exploits it.

**4. Comma splice → wrong.**
Two independent clauses joined by a comma alone.

**5. Colon without a complete sentence before it → wrong.**

These five checks kill an enormous number of choices instantly.

### Commas — the forbidden list

The ACT tests comma *overuse* more than underuse. Never place a comma:

| Never | Wrong example |
| --- | --- |
| Between subject and verb | *The team of researchers**,** published the study.* |
| Between verb and object | *She discovered**,** a new method.* |
| Between two verbs sharing a subject | *He wrote the paper**,** and submitted it.* |
| Before a closing dependent clause | *We left**,** because it was late.* |
| Around essential information | *The book**,** that I ordered**,** arrived.* |
| After a coordinating conjunction | *but**,** the results differed* |
| Between adjective and noun | *a difficult**,** problem* |
| Before a preposition beginning a needed phrase | *She walked**,** to the store.* |

### Essential vs. non-essential

| | Punctuation |
| --- | --- |
| **Essential** (identifies which one) | **No** commas |
| **Non-essential** (extra information) | Commas, dashes, or parentheses on **both** sides |

**The removal test:** delete the material. If the sentence still works and means
the same thing, it's non-essential and needs commas. If the sentence becomes
vague, it's essential and takes none.

> *My sister**,** who lives in Denver**,** is a nurse.* (one sister)
> *My sister who lives in Denver is a nurse.* (several sisters; this one)

**That vs. which:** *that* = essential, no comma. *which* = non-essential, comma.

### Apostrophes

| Form | Rule | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Singular possessive | `'s` | *the dog's leash* |
| Plural possessive | `'` | *the dogs' leashes* |
| Irregular plural possessive | `'s` | *the children's books* |
| Plural (no possession) | **nothing** | *three dogs* |
| Contraction | `'` replaces letters | *it's = it is* |

**Possessive pronouns never take apostrophes:** *its, hers, his, ours, yours,
theirs, whose.*

**The classic ACT question** offers four versions of the same noun:
*researchers* / *researcher's* / *researchers'* / *researchers's*.

Decide two things:
1. How many? (one → singular, more → plural)
2. Does it possess something? (yes → apostrophe, no → none)

### Dashes

- **One dash:** introduces an explanation, like a colon.
- **Two dashes:** set off an interruption, like paired commas.
- **Never mix:** a dash cannot pair with a comma.

### Colons

Require a **complete sentence before**. What follows can be anything.

> ✓ *She needed three things: time, money, and quiet.*
> ✗ *She needed: time, money, and quiet.*
> ✗ *The components are: soil, water, and light.*

The pattern of a colon after *are*, *include*, *such as*, or *for example* is a
recurring wrong answer.

## Sentence Structure and Formation

### Fragments

A fragment lacks a subject, lacks a finite verb, or is a dependent clause alone.

| Type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| No subject | *Ran the whole distance.* |
| No finite verb | *The team running the trial.* |
| Dependent alone | *Although the results were promising.* |
| Participial phrase alone | *Having reviewed the data.* |

**Key point:** an *-ing* word alone is **not** a finite verb. *The scientist
working late* is a fragment. *The scientist **was** working late* is a sentence.

The ACT builds fragments by replacing a main verb with a participle. Watch for
choices that turn `was studying` into `studying`.

### Run-ons and comma splices

**Five legal fixes:**

| Fix | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Period | *It rained. We stayed inside.* |
| Semicolon | *It rained; we stayed inside.* |
| Comma + FANBOYS | *It rained, so we stayed inside.* |
| Subordinate one clause | *Because it rained, we stayed inside.* |
| Colon (if explanatory) | *The reason was simple: it rained.* |

**Conjunctive adverbs are not conjunctions.** *However, therefore, moreover,
nevertheless, consequently, furthermore, thus, instead, otherwise* cannot join
independent clauses with a comma.

> ✗ *The data were incomplete, however the team proceeded.*
> ✓ *The data were incomplete; however, the team proceeded.*

This is tested on nearly every ACT English section.

### Modifiers

An opening phrase must modify the subject that immediately follows.

> ✗ *Having studied all night, the exam seemed easy.*
> ✓ *Having studied all night, **she** found the exam easy.*

**Method:** read the opening phrase, look at the very next noun, ask whether that
noun performed the action. If not, eliminate.

This check is fast and usually kills two choices at once.

**Misplaced limiting words** — *only, almost, just, nearly, even* — belong
immediately before what they limit.

### Parallelism

Items in a series or comparison share grammatical form.

> ✗ *hiking, swimming, and **to bike***
> ✓ *hiking, swimming, and **biking***

**Correlative pairs** must have matching structures after each half:
*not only ... but also* · *either ... or* · *neither ... nor* · *both ... and*

## Usage

### Subject-verb agreement

The ACT hides the subject. Cross out everything between it and the verb.

| Hiding device | Example | Subject |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prepositional phrase | *The **box** of nails **is** heavy* | box |
| Relative clause | *The **students** who took it **were** tired* | students |
| Appositive | ***Curie**, the physicist, **was**...* | Curie |
| Inversion | *Among the results **were** three anomalies* | three anomalies |
| There is/are | *There **are** several reasons* | several reasons |

**Rules:**
- *along with, as well as, in addition to, together with, including* don't
  change the subject's number
- *either/or*, *neither/nor* → agree with the **nearer** subject
- Collective nouns (*team, committee, group*) → singular
- *Each, every, either, neither, one, everyone, nobody* → singular
- Gerund subjects → singular

### Verb tense

Stay consistent with the passage unless a time marker signals a shift.

**Past perfect (*had* + participle)** for the earlier of two past events:
> *By the time help arrived, the fire **had spread**.*

**Watch irregular participles:** *had gone* not *had went*; *has taken* not *has
took*; *had begun* not *had began*; *had drunk*, *had swum*, *had written*.

### Pronouns

**Agreement:** match number. *Each student* → *his or her*. A *company* → *it*.

**Case:** subject (*I, he, she, we, they, who*) vs. object (*me, him, her, us,
them, whom*).

- **Compound test:** remove the other person. *Between you and **me**.*
- **Who/whom:** substitute *he/him*. *He* → *who*; *him* → *whom*.
- **Never** use a reflexive (*myself*) as a subject or plain object.

**Ambiguity:** if a pronoun could refer to two nouns, it's an error.

**The apostrophe set:** *its/it's*, *their/they're/there*, *whose/who's*,
*your/you're*. Tested on essentially every form.

### Adjective vs. adverb

Adverbs modify verbs; adjectives modify nouns.

> ✗ *She ran **quick**.* ✓ *She ran **quickly**.*

**Linking verbs** (*be, seem, appear, become, feel, look, sound, taste, smell*)
take **adjectives**:
> *The soup tastes **good**.* (not *well*)

### Comparisons

**Compare like things:**
> ✗ *The climate of Peru is milder than **Chile**.*
> ✓ *The climate of Peru is milder than **that of** Chile.*

**Comparative for two, superlative for three+:** *the taller of the two*, *the
tallest of the three*. Never *more taller*.

**Countable vs. uncountable:** *fewer/less*, *number/amount*, *many/much*.

## Strategy

### Vertical scanning

Look down the choices before reading the sentence. What varies tells you the
rule:

| Varies | Tested |
| --- | --- |
| Punctuation marks | Boundaries |
| `is`/`are` | Agreement |
| `its`/`it's` | Apostrophe |
| `runner's`/`runners'` | Possessive |
| `who`/`whom` | Case |
| Verb tenses | Consistency |
| Word order | Modifier placement |

### NO CHANGE

Correct roughly a quarter of the time. Do not avoid it. If you've applied the
rules and the original is correct, select it.

Students under-select NO CHANGE because it feels like giving up. That costs
points.

### Do not punctuate by ear

Your ear was trained on speech. Speech pauses where written English takes no
punctuation. If your reasoning is "there should be a pause there," you're
guessing.

Replace ear with procedure: identify the clauses, apply the join table.

## Traps

| Trap | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Subject-verb comma** | Long subject created a false pause |
| **Comma splice** | Two independents joined by a comma |
| **`However` as a conjunction** | Comma before *however* joining clauses |
| **One-sided interrupter** | Comma before, nothing after |
| **Plural with apostrophe** | *dog's* for multiple dogs |
| **Agreement with the nearest noun** | Verb matched the prepositional object |
| **Dangling modifier** | Opening phrase modifies the wrong subject |
| **Participle as main verb** | Created a fragment |
| **Avoided NO CHANGE** | Original was correct |
| **Adverb after a linking verb** | *feels badly* |

## Drill plan

Volume works here. The rules are few and they repeat constantly.

| Stage | Filter | Volume and method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Boundaries | Punctuation, Easy | 30 untimed. Write "IND" or "DEP" on each side. |
| 2. Commas | Punctuation, Easy → Medium | 30. Check against the forbidden list. |
| 3. Apostrophes | Punctuation + Usage, Easy | 20. Ask "how many" and "possesses what." |
| 4. Fragments and run-ons | Sentence Structure and Formation, Medium | 25 |
| 5. Agreement | Usage, Easy → Medium | 25. Cross out the middle every time. |
| 6. Modifiers and parallelism | Sentence Structure and Formation, Medium → Hard | 25 |
| 7. Mixed timed | Whole domain | 60 at **22 seconds each** |

Stage 7's target is the point of the whole plan. At 22 seconds per convention
question, you finish the mechanical half of ACT English in about seven minutes
total and have the rest of the section for rhetoric.
