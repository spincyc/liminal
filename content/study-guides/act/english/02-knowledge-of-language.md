# ACT English — Knowledge of Language

**Catalog domain:** Knowledge of Language
**Skill:** Effective Language Use
**~21% of the section** — about 11 questions

Precision, concision, style, and tone. These questions are grammatically
neutral: every choice is usually correct English. You're choosing the *best*
version.

Despite being the smallest domain, this is where the ACT's aesthetic shows most
clearly — and once you learn that aesthetic, these become fast, reliable points.

---

## Concision — the dominant principle

**When two choices are grammatically correct and mean the same thing, the
shorter one is credited.** The ACT treats wordiness as an error.

This single principle decides a large share of this domain.

### Redundancy

The most tested form of wordiness: saying the same thing twice.

**Redundant pairs:**

> annual yearly · past history · past experience · future plans · advance
> warning · advance planning · basic fundamentals · combine together · merge
> together · completely finish · each and every · end result · final outcome ·
> exact same · free gift · joint collaboration · new innovation · new
> beginning · personal opinion · repeat again · return back · revert back ·
> sudden impulse · sufficient enough · true fact · unexpected surprise · very
> unique · small in size · round in shape · blue in color · at 3 a.m. in the
> morning

**Redundancy across a sentence** is harder to spot and more commonly tested:

> ✗ *Because the bridge was unsafe, **the reason it closed was that it was
> dangerous**.*
> ✗ *The species is nocturnal, **meaning it is active at night, during the
> nighttime hours**.*
> ✗ *She arrived at 6 p.m., **which was the time of her arrival**.*

**Method:** read the full sentence and ask whether any information appears
twice. If the underlined portion repeats something stated elsewhere in the
sentence — or in the previous sentence — DELETE is likely correct.

### The DELETE / OMIT option

When *"DELETE the underlined portion"* or *"OMIT the underlined portion"*
appears, it is credited more often than 25% of the time.

**Take it when:**
- The material repeats information already present
- It's irrelevant to the sentence's point
- The sentence is complete and clear without it
- It's an empty intensifier or filler

**Don't take it when:**
- It provides new, necessary information
- It's grammatically required (a subject, a verb, a needed preposition)
- Removing it creates ambiguity
- It supplies a transition the passage needs

**Test:** read the sentence without the underlined portion. Is anything lost
besides words?

## Word choice and precision

### Choosing the most precise word

When choices are near-synonyms, the credited answer is the one that fits the
specific context most exactly.

Consider connotation and intensity:

| | | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *frugal* (positive) | *thrifty* (neutral) | *stingy* (negative) |
| *confident* | *assured* | *arrogant* |
| *determined* | *persistent* | *stubborn* |
| *curious* | *inquisitive* | *nosy* |
| *ambitious* | *driven* | *grasping* |
| *unusual* | *distinctive* | *bizarre* |

Match the passage's attitude toward the subject. A passage admiring a scientist
won't describe them as *obsessive*.

### Intensity matching

The word's strength should match the claim's strength.

> A study showing a modest correlation *suggests* a link; it does not *prove*
> one.
> A minor delay is an *inconvenience*, not a *catastrophe*.

### Idiomatic prepositions

Frequently tested. There's no rule — these are conventions.

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| account **for** | different **from** | preferable **to** | capable **of** |
| adhere **to** | independent **of** | prohibit **from** | comply **with** |
| agree **with** (person) | insist **on** | rely **on** | consist **of** |
| agree **to** (proposal) | interested **in** | responsible **for** | contrast **with** |
| apply **to** / **for** | native **to** | similar **to** | depend **on** |
| approve **of** | object **to** | subscribe **to** | wary **of** |
| associate **with** | opposed **to** | sympathize **with** | consistent **with** |

## Style and tone

### Consistency with the passage

The credited answer matches the passage's register. Most ACT passages are
moderately formal.

**Usually wrong:**
- Slang or casual constructions (*kind of*, *a lot of*, *totally*, *stuff*)
- Overly ornate or flowery language in a plain informational passage
- Contractions in a formal analytical passage
- Second-person *you* in a passage that doesn't otherwise address the reader

**Test:** does this sentence sound like it belongs in the same essay as the
sentences around it?

### Not the flowery one

Students frequently pick the most elaborate-sounding choice. The ACT prefers
clear, direct prose. Ornate phrasing is more often a distractor than an answer.

> ✗ *The scientist, in a manner both meticulous and unhurried, undertook the
> examination of the specimens.*
> ✓ *The scientist examined the specimens carefully.*

## Patterns and tells

**Shortest correct answer wins.** Check whether the shortest option is
grammatical and complete. If it is, it's probably right.

**DELETE deserves real consideration** every time it's offered.

**If the underlined portion repeats an idea from elsewhere in the sentence**,
the answer is usually DELETE or the shortest option.

**Watch for the "sounds sophisticated" trap.** Elaborate phrasing is a
distractor pattern.

**Preposition questions have exactly one idiomatic answer.** If you know it, it's
instant; if you don't, read each choice aloud mentally and pick the most
familiar.

**When all four choices are near-synonyms**, the question is about connotation
or precision. Reread the sentence for attitude and intensity.

---

## Traps

| Trap | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Elaborate = better** | Picked the longest, most formal-sounding option |
| **Missed redundancy** | Didn't notice the idea appeared earlier in the sentence |
| **Wrong connotation** | Right meaning, wrong emotional loading |
| **Over-strong word** | *Devastating* where *unfortunate* fits |
| **Register mismatch** | Casual phrasing in a formal passage |
| **Rejected DELETE reflexively** | Assumed deletion is too easy to be right |
| **Wrong preposition** | *Different than* instead of *different from* |

| 2. Concision | Effective Language Use, Easy → Medium | 25. **Always check the shortest choice first.** |

| 5. Idioms | Effective Language Use, all | 20. Build a list of every preposition you miss. |

memorization, and yours will be idiosyncratic.
