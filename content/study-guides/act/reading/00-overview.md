# ACT Reading — Overview

**36 questions · 40 minutes · four passages · 67 seconds per question**

One of the three Composite sections. For most students it's the hardest to pace
and the most frustrating to improve, because the difficulty is rarely about
comprehension — it's about speed and about resisting attractive wrong answers.

---

## Structure

Four passage sets, nine questions each, always in this order:

| # | Type | Content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Literary Narrative / Prose Fiction** | An excerpt from a novel or short story |
| 2 | **Social Science** | Economics, psychology, history, anthropology, sociology |
| 3 | **Humanities** | Art, music, literature, philosophy, memoir |
| 4 | **Natural Science** | Biology, chemistry, physics, earth science |

**One of the four is a paired set** — two shorter passages by different authors
on a related subject, with questions about each and about the relationship.

The enhanced format uses somewhat shorter passages with fewer questions each
than the older ACT, but the reading load remains the highest of any section on
either test.

### Domain weighting

| Domain | Share | Approx. |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| [Key Ideas and Details](01-key-ideas-and-details.md) | ~48% | 17-18 |
| [Craft and Structure](02-craft-and-structure.md) | ~28% | 10 |
| [Integration of Knowledge and Ideas](03-integration-of-knowledge-and-ideas.md) | ~24% | 8-9 |

**Nearly half the section is detail-location and inference.** Speed at finding
things in text is the core skill.

## Pacing — the binding constraint

**10 minutes per passage. Hard stop.**

| At | Finished |
| --- | --- |
| 10 min | Passage 1 |
| 20 min | Passage 2 |
| 30 min | Passage 3 |
| 40 min | Passage 4 |

Within a passage: roughly **3 minutes reading, 7 minutes on questions.**

**Enforce the stop.** Perfecting two questions on passage 2 while leaving three
blank on passage 4 is a losing trade — every question is worth the same. If
you're at 10 minutes, guess what's left and move.

### Passage order is yours to choose

You do not have to work in order. If one passage type is reliably your worst, do
it **last**, so if anything gets shortchanged it's the one you'd score lowest on
anyway.

Common choice: students who struggle with Prose Fiction do passages 2-4 first,
then return. Students who struggle with Natural Science do the opposite.

Decide this in practice, not on test day, and then always do it the same way.

## Three reading approaches

Pick one in practice. Do not switch on test day.

### A. Read then answer

Skim the passage in ~3 minutes for structure, main idea, and tone. Don't try to
retain details — note *where* things are. Then answer, returning to the text for
specifics.

**Best for:** fast readers, and for Prose Fiction and Humanities where tone and
overall meaning matter.

### B. Skim then hunt

Read the first paragraph, the last paragraph, and the first sentence of each
middle paragraph (~90 seconds). Then go to the questions and locate each answer
in the text.

**Best for:** slow readers who can't finish, and for Natural Science and Social
Science where questions are detail-heavy and line-referenced.

### C. Questions first

Read the questions (not the choices) first, then read the passage hunting for
those specifics.

**Best for:** students who lose focus reading cold. Risky — it's easy to burn
time re-reading.

**Most students land on A or B.** If you consistently run out of time, switch to
B. If you consistently finish with time left but miss detail questions, switch
to A.

## The universal method for each question

1. **Read the question stem.** Identify the type: detail, inference, main idea,
   vocabulary, function, comparison.
2. **Go back to the text.** Even when you remember. Especially when you
   remember.
3. **Read enough context** — the referenced line plus the sentence before and
   after. Answers frequently depend on the surrounding sentence.
4. **Predict** in your own words.
5. **Eliminate** against your prediction, with a reason for each cross-off.
6. **Verify** — point to the words that support your choice.

Step 2 is the one that separates scores. The ACT's distractors are built to
match your *memory* of the passage. Going back to the text defeats them.

---

## The evidence standard

> Every correct answer is provable from the text.

Not "reasonable." Not "probably true." **Provable.** If you cannot put your
finger on the words that support a choice, it's wrong.

This is the standard that makes ACT Reading tractable. It also means your own
knowledge is a liability: a choice that's true in the real world but unstated in
the passage is a distractor, and it's specifically there to catch students who
know the subject.

## What the wrong answers look like

| Type | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **True but not stated** | Accurate in reality, absent from the passage |
| **Stated but irrelevant** | In the passage, doesn't answer this question |
| **Too extreme** | Right direction, overstated with *always*, *never*, *proves* |
| **Too broad / too narrow** | Wrong scope |
| **Half-right** | First clause correct, second false |
| **Word-match trap** | Repeats passage vocabulary, distorts the claim |
| **Wrong part of the passage** | Answers a question about a different paragraph |
| **Reversal** | Attributes a view to the wrong person, or flips the relationship |

The **word-match trap** is the most dangerous under time pressure. When you're
rushing, vocabulary overlap feels like confirmation. Item writers build
distractors from passage words arranged into false statements precisely because
of that.

## Passage-type strategy

### Prose Fiction / Literary Narrative

Questions are about **character, relationship, motivation, and tone**, not plot
facts.

- Track **who feels what about whom**, and how that changes.
- Attitude words matter more than events.
- **Don't over-infer emotion.** The credited answer is supported by specific
  language, not by what you'd feel in that situation.
- Dialogue frequently reveals character indirectly — note what a character
  *avoids* saying.

Many students find this the hardest passage. If that's you, do it last.

### Social Science

Usually an argument or an explanation of a phenomenon.

- Identify the **author's claim** and the **evidence**.
- Watch for **multiple viewpoints** — the author's versus one being described.
  Attributing a described view to the author is a top error here.
- Dates, names, and studies are detail-question fodder. Note where they are.

### Humanities

Often a memoir, a profile, or arts criticism.

- Tone and **the author's stance toward the subject** are heavily tested.
- Distinguish the **author's view** from **the view of the person described**.
- Figurative language questions are common here.

### Natural Science

Usually a process, a study, or a scientific debate.

- **You don't need the science.** Everything required is in the passage.
- Track **cause and effect** relationships and the **sequence** of a process.
- Questions are the most literal of the four passages — location skills matter
  more than interpretation.

Often the best passage to do first if you're comfortable with it: the questions
are findable, which builds momentum.

---

## Paired passages

One set has two passages. Strategy:

1. Read **Passage A**, answer the questions about A only.
2. Read **Passage B**, answer the questions about B only.
3. Answer the **comparison** questions last.

Do not read both before answering anything — you'll blur them together, and
mixing up which author said what is the dominant error on these.

Before the comparison questions, state each author's position in one sentence.
