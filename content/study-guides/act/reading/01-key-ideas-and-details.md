# ACT Reading — Key Ideas and Details

**Catalog domain:** Key Ideas and Details
**Skills:** Central Ideas, Themes, and Summaries · Relationships · Inferences and
Conclusions · Supporting Details
**~48% of the section** — 17-18 questions

Nearly half of ACT Reading. If you improve one domain, make it this one.

---

## Supporting Details

The most common question type on the section. Also the fastest, once you can
locate text quickly.

### Question forms

- "According to the passage, [X] occurred because..."
- "The passage states that..."
- "As described in the passage, [X] is..."
- "Which of the following is NOT mentioned in the passage?"

### Method

1. **Identify the key term** in the question — a name, a date, a concept.
2. **Scan for it in the passage.** Don't re-read; scan for the specific word.
3. Read that sentence **and its neighbors**.
4. Match a choice to what the text says.

### Speed technique: scanning, not reading

Detail questions are found by **visual scanning for a distinctive word**, not by
re-reading. Names, numbers, italicized terms, and capitalized words are visually
distinctive — your eye can find them in seconds.

If the question's key term is generic (*the author's argument*), scan instead
for the paragraph you remember it being in.

**This skill is trainable and it's mostly what separates fast from slow test
takers.** Practice it deliberately: give yourself 15 seconds to locate a term in
a passage, repeatedly.

### "EXCEPT" and "NOT" questions

> "All of the following are mentioned EXCEPT..."

These take longer because you must verify **three** choices, not one.

**Method:** find each of the four in the passage. Three will be there. The one
you can't find is the answer.

Do not try to shortcut by intuition. Check all four. These are worth the extra
30 seconds because they're otherwise reliably gettable.

### Patterns

**Detail answers are close paraphrases.** Unlike inference questions, near-
verbatim matching is a *good* sign here.

**The answer is usually within one sentence of the key term** — but check the
next sentence too, because qualifications live there.

**Watch for negation and qualification.** The passage says a method "rarely
succeeded"; a choice saying it "did not succeed" is different and wrong.

### Traps

| Trap | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Right word, wrong claim** | Uses passage vocabulary to state something false |
| **Answer from the wrong paragraph** | The term appears twice; you found the wrong instance |
| **Dropped qualifier** | Passage says "in most cases"; choice says universally |
| **Combined details** | Two true facts joined into a false relationship |
| **Plausible outside knowledge** | True, not in the passage |

## Central Ideas, Themes, and Summaries

### Question forms

- "The main idea of the passage is that..."
- "The main purpose of the [second paragraph] is to..."
- "Which of the following best summarizes the passage?"
- "The passage is best described as..."

### Method

1. Determine whether the question is about the **whole passage** or **one
   paragraph**. Getting this wrong is a common, avoidable error.
2. State the main idea in your own words, in under ten words.
3. Eliminate choices that are details.
4. Eliminate choices broader than the passage.
5. Verify the remaining choice covers the **whole** scope asked about,
   including the ending.

### Patterns

**The credited answer accounts for the entire passage**, including the last
paragraph. Distractors accurately summarize the first two-thirds and stop before
a shift.

**Paragraph purpose questions are about function**, not content. Common
functions: introduce, provide background, give an example, present a
counterargument, qualify, conclude, transition.

**For Prose Fiction, "main idea" is usually about a relationship or a
realization**, not about events. The plot is the vehicle.

**Tone-inclusive summaries:** if the passage is critical, a neutral summary is
incomplete. Match the attitude as well as the content.

### Traps

| Trap | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Detail as main idea** | A true supporting fact presented as the point |
| **Scope too broad** | Passage covers one case; choice covers the field |
| **Ignores the pivot** | Misses a shift in the last paragraph |
| **Wrong paragraph** | Answers about the passage when one paragraph was asked |
| **Tone mismatch** | Content right, attitude wrong |

## Relationships

Questions about how things in the passage connect: cause and effect, sequence,
comparison, character relationships.

### Question forms

- "According to the passage, what caused [X]?"
- "The passage suggests that [X] and [Y] are related in that..."
- "Which of the following best describes the relationship between the narrator
  and her grandmother?"
- "The events in the passage occur in which order?"

### Method

1. **Name the relationship type** the question is asking about: causal,
   temporal, comparative, or interpersonal.
2. Find where both elements appear in the text.
3. Determine the direction — **which causes which**, which came first.
4. Match.

### Patterns

**Direction is the trap.** Reversed causation and reversed chronology are the
most common wrong answers on relationship questions. After selecting, spend
three seconds confirming the direction.

**Correlation vs. causation applies here too.** If the passage says two things
happened together, a choice asserting one caused the other is wrong unless the
text says so.

**For character relationships**, the answer is supported by specific actions and
dialogue, not by a general impression. Find the line.

**Comparison questions** require both sides. A choice describing only one
element is wrong.

### Traps

| Trap | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Reversed causation** | X caused Y, choice says Y caused X |
| **Reversed chronology** | Order of events flipped |
| **Invented cause** | A plausible mechanism the passage doesn't state |
| **One-sided comparison** | Describes one element, ignores the other |
| **Over-strong relationship** | "Depends entirely on" when the text says "influenced by" |

## Inferences and Conclusions

The hardest skill in the domain.

### Question forms

- "It can reasonably be inferred from the passage that..."
- "The passage most strongly suggests that..."
- "The author would most likely agree that..."
- "Based on the passage, [X] can best be described as..."

### The standard: supported, not merely possible

An ACT inference is **small**. Usually one step from something explicitly
stated. The credited answer is the one the text *supports*, not the one a
reasonable person would guess.

**The test:** *"Can I point to the specific words that make this necessary?"*

If your justification requires a chain of reasoning or an assumption the passage
doesn't make, it's wrong.

### Method

1. Locate the relevant text.
2. State what it explicitly says.
3. Ask what follows **immediately** from that.
4. For each choice, ask: could the passage be entirely true and this choice
   still be false? If yes → eliminate.
5. Verify the survivor against specific words.

### Patterns

**Hedged answers win.** *Some*, *may*, *tends to*, *at least in part*. An
absolute inference over-reaches.

**"The author would most likely agree" means using the author's stated
positions**, not inventing a plausible opinion. Find where the author states a
view, and extend it minimally.

**Distinguish the author from described views.** In Social Science and
Humanities passages, the author often describes a position they don't hold.
Attributing it to them is a top error. Look for the author's own evaluative
language: *however*, *but this overlooks*, *convincingly*, *unfortunately*.

**Negative inferences are common** — "X is not sufficient to explain Y," "the
relationship is more complex than assumed." They're safe, which is why they're
credited.

### Traps

| Trap | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Plausible extension** | Sensible, unsupported |
| **Too big a leap** | Requires two or three inferential steps |
| **Author/subject confusion** | Attributes a described view to the author |
| **Extreme phrasing** | Right idea, absolute language |
| **Outside knowledge** | True in the world, not in the text |
| **Reversal** | Correct relationship, wrong direction |

### Worked reasoning

> *Passage excerpt (paraphrased): The author describes a nineteenth-century
> naturalist who catalogued thousands of specimens but published almost nothing,
> noting that his contemporaries dismissed him as unproductive. The author then
> observes that modern researchers, working from his preserved collections, have
> identified dozens of species that would otherwise have gone unrecorded.*
>
> **It can reasonably be inferred that the author believes the naturalist's
> contemporaries...**

*What's explicitly stated:* contemporaries dismissed him as unproductive; his
collections later proved scientifically valuable.

*What follows immediately:* the contemporaries' judgment was, in hindsight,
mistaken or at least incomplete.

Evaluate shapes:

- **"were correct in their assessment"** — contradicted by the second half.
- **"were jealous of his collecting abilities"** — invented motive. Nothing
  supports envy.
- **"underestimated the value of his work"** — one step from the stated
  contrast. Hedged, minimal, provable. **Credited shape.**
- **"should have recognized that cataloguing is more important than
  publishing"** — a normative claim the author doesn't make, and stronger than
  the text supports.

Notice the credited answer is the smallest possible step. That's the pattern.

## Drill plan

| Stage | Filter | Volume and method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Location speed | Supporting Details, Easy | 30. Time yourself finding the key term — target under 15 seconds. |
| 2. Detail accuracy | Supporting Details, Medium | 25. Underline the supporting words for every answer. |
| 3. EXCEPT questions | Supporting Details, Medium → Hard | 15. Verify all four every time. |
| 4. Main idea | Central Ideas, Themes, and Summaries, Medium | 20. Write a 10-word summary before looking. |
| 5. Relationships | Relationships, Medium | 20. State the direction explicitly. |
| 6. Inference | Inferences and Conclusions, Medium → Hard | 30. **Write why each eliminated choice could be false.** |
| 7. Mixed timed | Whole domain | Two full passages at 10 min each |

Stage 1 is the highest-yield and the most neglected. Detail-location speed is a
mechanical skill, it's the largest question type on the section, and 20 minutes
of deliberate practice a day for a week measurably improves it.

Stage 6's write-it-down instruction is slow and it's the only thing that builds
the must/could reflex. Do it for at least 15 items before letting yourself do it
mentally.
