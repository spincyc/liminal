## Word Meaning and Word Choice

### Question forms

- "As it is used in line 34, the word *temper* most nearly means..."
- "The phrase *a thin margin* (line 12) most nearly refers to..."
- "In context, the author's use of the word *merely* suggests..."

### This is a context test, not a vocabulary test

The tested word is usually common, and the tested meaning is frequently **not**
the most familiar one. Knowing the dictionary definition can actively hurt.

### Method

1. **Go to the line.** Always. Never answer from memory.
2. **Cover the choices.**
3. Read the sentence, plus the one before and after.
4. **Insert your own word.** Anything that fits — plain words are fine.
5. Uncover and match.
6. **Plug the choice back in** and reread the sentence.

Step 2 is the technique. Every distractor is a real meaning of the word,
plausible in isolation. A prediction is what protects you.

### Context clue types

| Clue | Signal | Tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition | appositive, dashes, *that is* | The meaning is nearby |
| Contrast | *but*, *however*, *unlike*, *whereas*, *despite* | Opposite of a nearby idea |
| Continuation | *and*, *moreover*, *similarly* | Similar to a nearby idea |
| Cause/effect | *because*, *so*, *therefore* | Fits a causal chain |
| Example | *for instance*, *such as* | Examples define the category |

### Patterns

**The most common meaning is usually a distractor.** If the word is ordinary,
ask why it's being tested — probably because a secondary sense is in play.

Words with heavily tested secondary meanings: *appreciate* (increase in value),
*qualify* (limit), *arrest* (stop), *novel* (new), *sound* (valid), *just*
(fair/merely), *check* (restrain), *compromise* (weaken), *temper* (moderate),
*charge* (accusation/responsibility), *bear* (carry/endure), *reserve*
(restraint), *table* (postpone), *fast* (firmly fixed), *object* (protest).

**Register and connotation matter.** Match the passage's formality and its
attitude.

**Figurative language questions** ask what an image conveys, not what it
literally describes. The answer is the *point* of the metaphor.

### Traps

| Trap | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Common meaning** | The usual sense, wrong here |
| **Right idea, wrong intensity** | *Furious* where *annoyed* fits |
| **Wrong connotation** | Positive word in a critical context |
| **Literal reading of a figure** | Explained the image instead of its meaning |
| **Grammatically incompatible** | Wrong part of speech or preposition |

## Text Structure

### Question forms

- "The main function of the third paragraph is to..."
- "Which of the following best describes the structure of the passage?"
- "The author's discussion of [X] in lines 20-28 primarily serves to..."
- "The passage can best be characterized as..."

### Function questions

You're asked what a paragraph or sentence **does**, not what it says.

**Method:**

1. Read the referenced material.
2. Read what comes **immediately before and after** — function is defined by
   relationship.
3. Describe the job in plain words: *"it gives an example of the problem the
   last paragraph described."*
4. Match.

**Common functions and how answers phrase them:**

| Function | Answer phrasing |
| --- | --- |
| Introduce | "introduce a phenomenon the passage goes on to explain" |
| Provide background | "supply context necessary to understand the debate" |
| Give an example | "illustrate the principle described earlier" |
| Provide evidence | "offer support for the claim in the preceding paragraph" |
| Raise an objection | "present a limitation of the approach" |
| Qualify | "acknowledge a constraint on the finding" |
| Contrast | "offer an alternative to the view just described" |
| Conclude | "state a conclusion drawn from the preceding discussion" |
| Transition | "shift from description to evaluation" |

**Pattern:** distractors are frequently accurate *content descriptions* rather
than *function statements*. If a choice tells you what the paragraph says
instead of what it accomplishes, be suspicious. Credited answers usually contain
a relational verb.

### Whole-passage structure

Common shapes in ACT passages:

- **Narrative:** a sequence of events, often with a reflective frame
- **Problem → attempted solutions → assessment**
- **Old understanding → new evidence → revised understanding**
- **General claim → supporting cases → qualification**
- **Description of a person → their significance**
- **Two competing explanations → evaluation**

**Method:** summarize each paragraph in three or four words, then read your map.

**Pattern:** the credited answer accounts for the **whole** passage including the
final paragraph. Distractors describe the opening accurately and miss a shift.

## Purpose and Point of View

### Question forms

- "The author's attitude toward [X] can best be described as..."
- "The main purpose of the passage is to..."
- "The narrator's tone in the final paragraph is best described as..."
- "The passage is written from the point of view of someone who..."

### Attitude and tone

**Method:**

1. Find the author's or narrator's **evaluative language** — adjectives,
   adverbs, and verbs that carry judgment.
2. Determine the **valence**: positive, negative, or neutral.
3. Determine the **intensity**: mild or strong.
4. Match on both dimensions.

**The two-dimension check eliminates fast.** If the passage is mildly positive,
you can kill every negative choice and every strongly positive choice
immediately.

### The moderation principle

**ACT authors are rarely extreme.** Published academic and literary prose is
measured. Attitude answers skew toward:

> *measured · qualified · appreciative · skeptical · ambivalent · curious ·
> respectful but critical · cautiously optimistic*

and away from:

> *contemptuous · outraged · euphoric · dismissive · hostile · indifferent*

**Extreme attitude words are usually wrong.** So is *indifferent* — an author who
wrote a passage about something is rarely indifferent to it.

**Fails when:** the passage genuinely is polemical. Some Humanities passages are
sharply critical. Check the language rather than applying the rule blindly.

### Useful tone vocabulary

**Positive:** admiring · appreciative · enthusiastic · optimistic · sympathetic ·
respectful · affectionate · celebratory

**Negative:** critical · skeptical · dismissive · disapproving · wary ·
disillusioned · resigned · impatient

**Neutral / mixed:** objective · analytical · detached · measured · ambivalent ·
nostalgic · wistful · reflective · bemused · wry · candid · matter-of-fact

Be able to define each. Attitude questions frequently hinge on knowing that
*wry* is amused rather than bitter, or that *ambivalent* means mixed rather than
indifferent.

### Author vs. subject — the critical distinction

Passages routinely describe views the author does not hold.

**Signals of the author's own voice:**

| Signal | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Evaluative adverbs | *convincingly*, *unfortunately*, *remarkably* |
| Pivot words | *But this account overlooks...* |
| Hedges on a described view | *supposedly*, *purportedly*, *it is often claimed* |
| Direct assertion | *The evidence in fact suggests...* |

**Signals of a described view:** *proponents argue*, *according to*, *critics
maintain*, *the traditional account holds*, *some researchers believe*.

Attributing a described position to the author is one of the most common errors
in this domain. When a question asks about the author, find the sentences where
the author is speaking in their own voice.

### Purpose questions

"The main purpose of the passage is to..." — the answer is a **verb of
intention**:

> to describe · to explain · to argue · to compare · to trace the development
> of · to challenge · to illustrate · to profile · to evaluate

**Match the verb first.** A passage that explains a process is not *arguing*. A
passage that profiles a person is not *evaluating a theory*. Choosing on the
verb alone eliminates two or three choices on most of these.

Then check the object of the verb for scope.

### Point of view

For Prose Fiction: is the narrator a participant or an observer? First person or
third? Do they have full access to characters' thoughts or only one character's?

Questions asking "the passage is written from the point of view of someone who"
are answered by what the narrator **can know and does notice**, not by their
personality.

## Traps

| Trap | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Author/subject confusion** | Attributed a described view to the author |
| **Extreme attitude** | *Contemptuous* where *skeptical* fits |
| **Indifferent** | Rarely correct |
| **Content instead of function** | Described what a paragraph says, not what it does |
| **Wrong purpose verb** | *Argue* for a descriptive passage |
| **Missed the final shift** | Structure answer ignores the last paragraph |
| **Dictionary definition** | Used the common meaning of a tested word |
| **Literal reading of figurative language** | |

## Drill plan

| Stage | Filter | Volume and method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Vocabulary in context | Word Meaning and Word Choice, Easy → Hard | 30. **Cover choices, write a prediction** for the first 20. |
| 2. Function | Text Structure, Medium | 20. Write the job in your own words first. |
| 3. Structure | Text Structure, Medium → Hard | 15. Map each paragraph in four words. |
| 4. Attitude | Purpose and Point of View, Medium | 25. Record valence and intensity separately. |
| 5. Author vs. subject | Purpose and Point of View, Hard | 15. Highlight the author's own-voice sentences. |
| 6. Purpose | Purpose and Point of View, Medium | 15. Choose on the verb first. |

Stage 4's two-dimension habit — valence, then intensity — is the fastest route
to reliable attitude questions. Most students evaluate holistically and get
talked into an over-strong choice.
