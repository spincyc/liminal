# ACT Writing — Essay Method

**Catalog domains:** Ideas and Analysis · Development and Support

The two domains that measure your thinking. This file is about generating a
thesis and building real analysis under time pressure.

---

## The planning phase (4 minutes)

### Step 1 — Read all three perspectives and find the axis

The three perspectives are not random. They almost always fall along a spectrum
or represent distinct values in tension. Identify what the disagreement is
*about*.

Typical patterns:

| Pattern | Shape |
| --- | --- |
| **Spectrum** | One extreme, one moderate, one opposite extreme |
| **Values conflict** | Efficiency vs. equity, freedom vs. safety, progress vs. tradition |
| **Scope disagreement** | Same value, different judgments about how far it extends |
| **Different framings** | Economic vs. moral vs. practical |

Naming the axis takes fifteen seconds and it makes everything downstream easier,
because your thesis can locate itself on that axis rather than floating free.

### Step 2 — Choose your thesis

**Three viable approaches, in ascending difficulty:**

**A. Agree with one perspective, with a qualification.**
Simplest and entirely sufficient for a strong score. Pick the perspective you
can best support, then add a condition or limit.

> *Perspective Two is largely correct — automation does create new categories of
> work — but only where displaced workers have real access to retraining.*

**B. Synthesize two perspectives.**
Argue that two of them are compatible under specified conditions.

> *Perspectives One and Three are usually framed as opposed, but they describe
> different timescales: One is right about the immediate disruption, Three about
> the long-run adjustment.*

**C. Reframe the debate.**
Argue that all three share a flawed assumption. Highest ceiling, highest risk —
if you can't execute it, the essay reads as evasive.

> *All three perspectives treat efficiency as the relevant measure. The more
> useful question is who bears the cost of the transition.*

**Pick A unless B or C comes to you immediately.** A well-executed A scores
higher than a muddled C. Do not spend planning time hunting for cleverness.

**The critical requirement, whichever you choose:** your position must be
**specific and arguable**. "There are good points on both sides" is not a
thesis.

### Step 3 — Map your relationship to each perspective

For each of the three, note in a few words:

- What it gets **right**
- What it **misses or assumes**
- How **your** position relates to it

This map *is* your essay's analytical content. Doing it in planning means you
never have to invent analysis while writing.

### Step 4 — Pick two or three concrete examples

Specific beats general. Sources that work:

| Source | Example |
| --- | --- |
| History | A specific policy, event, or period |
| Current events | A named company, law, or trend |
| Science and technology | A specific development and its effects |
| Literature | A work and what it illustrates |
| Personal or local experience | A specific situation you observed |

**Personal experience is fully acceptable** and often more vivid than a
half-remembered historical example. What matters is **specificity** — details,
consequences, and a clear connection to your claim.

**Do not fabricate statistics or studies.** Invented precision reads as invented.
Reason from what you actually know.

## Analysis: the skill that moves the score

The difference between a 6 and a 9 is almost entirely here.

### Summary versus analysis

> **Summary:** "Perspective One says that automation eliminates jobs and harms
> workers."
>
> **Analysis:** "Perspective One treats job elimination as the end of the story,
> which assumes displaced workers cannot move into new roles. That assumption is
> reasonable when the new roles require credentials the displaced workers lack —
> but it understates how often the constraint is access to retraining rather
> than capability."

The second one **examines the assumption**, **states the condition under which
it holds**, and **identifies what it understates**.

### Four analytical moves

Learn these four. Any one of them turns summary into analysis.

**1. Name the assumption.**
> *This perspective assumes that ___.*

Every position rests on unstated premises. Surfacing one is the single fastest
route to analysis.

**2. Specify the conditions.**
> *This holds when ___, but breaks down when ___.*

Most perspectives are right in some circumstances. Saying *which* is
sophisticated reasoning and it's easy to execute.

**3. Trace the implication.**
> *If we accept this, it follows that ___ — which the perspective does not
> address.*

Following a position to its logical consequence, especially an uncomfortable
one, is high-value analysis.

**4. Identify the blind spot.**
> *This perspective focuses on ___ and therefore does not account for ___.*

What the perspective leaves out, and why leaving it out matters.

### Using the moves

You don't need all four. **Two well-executed moves across the essay is
plenty.** Use one on the perspective you're partly agreeing with and one on the
perspective you're arguing against.

Applying a move takes two or three sentences. Practice them until they're
reflexive — you should be able to produce "this assumes that..." without
thinking about it.

## Development and Support

The second domain: are your claims actually reasoned through, or just asserted?

### The chain

Every body paragraph should run:

```
Claim  →  Reasoning  →  Evidence  →  Significance
```

| Element | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Claim** | The topic sentence. What this paragraph argues. |
| **Reasoning** | *Why* the claim is true. The logical step. |
| **Evidence** | A concrete example. |
| **Significance** | Why this matters for your thesis. |

**The most commonly skipped links are reasoning and significance.** Students
write a claim, drop an example, and move on — leaving the grader to infer the
connection. Say it explicitly.

### Developing an example

A well-developed example is not longer, it's more *connected*:

> **Underdeveloped:** "For example, the printing press changed society."
>
> **Developed:** "The printing press illustrates this. Its immediate effect was
> to displace scribes, whose skill was suddenly worth far less. But it also
> created demand for printers, editors, and eventually authors who could earn a
> living from writing — roles that did not previously exist. The displacement
> was real; so was the expansion. What made the difference was that the new
> roles were accessible to people with adjacent skills."

Notice: same example, but the second version explains the mechanism and ties it
to the argument.

**One well-developed example beats three mentioned in passing.**

### Addressing the counterargument

Engaging the strongest opposing view is directly rewarded, and it's one of the
clearest markers of a high-scoring essay.

**Structure:**

> *The strongest objection to this is ___. That objection has force because ___.
> But it underestimates ___.*

The middle sentence matters. Acknowledging that the objection is *reasonable*
before answering it reads as genuine engagement rather than a rhetorical move.

Since you must engage all three perspectives anyway, the perspective you most
disagree with is your natural counterargument.

## A worked planning example

> **Issue:** Should schools replace letter grades with narrative feedback?
>
> **Perspective One:** Letter grades are efficient and let institutions compare
> students fairly.
> **Perspective Two:** Grades reduce learning to a number and discourage
> intellectual risk-taking.
> **Perspective Three:** Narrative feedback is more informative but impractical
> at scale.

**Axis:** efficiency and comparability versus depth of feedback. Perspective
Three is a practical objection rather than a value position.

**Thesis (approach A, agree with qualification):** Perspective Two identifies a
real cost, and it's the one that matters most — but the fix isn't abolishing
grades, it's reducing how much rides on any single one.

**Map:**

| Perspective | Gets right | Misses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One | Comparability genuinely matters for admissions and hiring | Assumes comparability requires *frequent* grading |
| Two | Grades do discourage risk-taking | Doesn't address what replaces the comparison function |
| Three | Scale is a real constraint | Treats it as binary; partial adoption is possible |

**Examples:** pass/fail systems in some professional schools; the observable
behavior change when a course drops a graded midterm; my own experience choosing
an easier elective to protect a GPA.

**Analytical moves:** name Perspective One's assumption (comparability requires
frequent grading); specify conditions for Perspective Three (scale is a
constraint on *full* adoption, not on partial).

That plan took about four minutes and the essay is now mostly transcription.

## Common failures

| Failure | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| **Summarizing perspectives** | Apply an analytical move to each |
| **No clear thesis** | State your position in the introduction, specifically |
| **Ignoring a perspective** | Address all three; the task requires it |
| **Assertion without reasoning** | Add the "because" |
| **Generic examples** | Add specifics: names, dates, mechanisms |
| **Fence-sitting** | "Both sides have merit" is not a position |
| **Fabricated evidence** | Reason from what you know |
| **Running out of time** | Plan first; write from the plan |

## Practice protocol

**Weeks 1-2 — untimed.** Write two full essays with no clock. Focus entirely on
analysis: for each, mark where you used one of the four analytical moves. If
there are fewer than two marks, revise.

**Week 3 — planning drills.** Take five prompts and write **only the plan** —
axis, thesis, three-perspective map, examples, analytical moves. Four minutes
each. This is the highest-value drill available, because planning is the
bottleneck.

**Week 4 — timed.** Two full essays at 40 minutes, handwritten, with the real
planning budget.

**Throughout:** reread your essays 24 hours later and score them against the
four domains honestly. Ask specifically: *did I analyze, or did I summarize?*
