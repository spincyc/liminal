# ACT Writing — Structure and Language

**Catalog domains:** Organization · Language Use and Conventions

Two of the four scoring domains. Less differentiating than
[Ideas and Analysis](01-essay-method.md), but they're half your score and
they're the easiest half to secure.

---

## Organization

### A structure that works

This is a reliable shape, not a mandatory template. Adapt it.

| Paragraph | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Introduction** (3-5 sentences) | Frame the issue in one sentence, name the tension, state your thesis clearly |
| **Body 1** | Engage the perspective closest to yours; analyze it; support your position |
| **Body 2** | Engage the perspective you most disagree with; acknowledge its force; explain what it misses |
| **Body 3** | The third perspective, or a deeper development of your own position with your strongest example |
| **Conclusion** (2-4 sentences) | Restate the thesis in new words; state the implication |

Three body paragraphs is comfortable in 28 minutes of writing. Two
well-developed ones beat three thin ones.

### The introduction

**Do:**
- Get to the issue in the first sentence
- Name the genuine tension in one sentence
- **State your thesis specifically and unmistakably**

**Don't:**
- Open with "In today's society..." or "Throughout human history..."
- Restate the prompt at length
- Save your position for the conclusion

> *Automation has eliminated categories of work before, and each time the
> question has been whether displacement is a transition or a permanent loss.
> Perspective Two argues that new roles reliably emerge. That is usually true —
> but only where displaced workers have real access to the training the new
> roles require, and that access is exactly what tends to be missing.*

Four sentences. The issue, the tension, the position, and the qualification.
A grader knows exactly what this essay will argue.

### Body paragraphs

Each one runs:

```
Topic sentence  →  Engage the perspective  →  Reasoning  →  Evidence  →  Significance
```

**The topic sentence must state the paragraph's argument**, not its subject.

> ✗ *Perspective One discusses efficiency.*
> ✓ *Perspective One is right that comparability matters, but wrong to assume it
> requires frequent grading.*

The second version tells the grader what the paragraph will do. Since graders
read fast, visible topic sentences directly serve you.

### Ordering

Two workable sequences:

**Weakest to strongest** — build toward your best material. Ends with impact.

**Address opposition, then build** — handle the perspective you disagree with
early, then develop your own. Clears the objection out of the way.

Either works. What doesn't work is random order, or discussing perspectives in
the order given without a reason.

### The conclusion

Two to four sentences. Do:

- Restate the thesis **in different words**
- Note the implication — what follows if you're right

Don't:
- Introduce a new argument
- Repeat the introduction verbatim
- Say "In conclusion" and then summarize each paragraph

**If you're running out of time, write two sentences.** A short conclusion is
far better than none — an essay that stops mid-paragraph reads as incomplete and
costs you in Organization.

### Transitions

Transitions connect *ideas*, not paragraphs. The best ones reference the
previous point's content:

> *This objection has force, but it depends on a condition that rarely holds.*

That's better than "Furthermore," because it carries meaning.

**Useful moves:**

| Purpose | Phrasing |
| --- | --- |
| Contrast | *However* · *Yet* · *On the other hand* · *This overlooks* |
| Concession | *Admittedly* · *To be sure* · *This is not to deny that* |
| Cause | *Consequently* · *As a result* · *It follows that* |
| Addition | *Moreover* · *Beyond this* · *A further consideration* |
| Example | *Consider* · *For instance* · *This is visible in* |
| Return to thesis | *This is precisely why* · *The point is not that ___ but that ___* |

Vary them. Four paragraphs each beginning "Furthermore" reads mechanically.

## Language Use and Conventions

The fourth domain: sentence structure, word choice, and correctness.

### Sentence variety

**This is the most under-attended element of essay prose.** Graders notice
monotony even when they can't name it.

Vary:

- **Length.** Mix long, complex sentences with short, direct ones. **A short
  sentence after a long one lands hard.** Use that deliberately at the end of a
  paragraph.
- **Openings.** Not every sentence should start with the subject. Open sometimes
  with a subordinate clause (*Because the constraint is access rather than
  capability, ...*), a prepositional phrase (*In manufacturing, ...*), or a
  participle (*Framed this way, ...*).
- **Structure.** Simple, compound, and complex sentences.

A quick self-check: read a paragraph and look at the first three words of each
sentence. If they're all the same shape, revise one.

### Word choice

**Precision beats sophistication.**

- Use the word you're sure of. A misused advanced word costs more than a plain
  correct one gains.
- Prefer concrete nouns and strong verbs to abstract nouns and weak verbs.
  *"Automation displaces workers"* beats *"there is a displacement effect on the
  workforce."*
- Cut intensifiers that add nothing: *very, really, extremely, incredibly*.
- Avoid clichés: *at the end of the day, the fact of the matter is, since the
  dawn of time*.

**Useful analytical vocabulary** — words worth having ready because they do real
work in this essay:

> assumes · presupposes · overlooks · understates · conflates · distinguishes ·
> holds only when · follows from · at the cost of · in practice · the underlying
> question · the relevant measure · a necessary but not sufficient condition

These aren't decoration. Each one names an analytical move.

### Register

Formal but not stiff.

- **First person is fine** and often necessary — you're stating your own
  perspective. *"I argue that"* and *"In my view"* are appropriate.
- Avoid contractions in formal writing, though this is minor.
- Avoid slang and conversational filler.
- Don't address the reader as *you*.

### Conventions

A first draft in 40 minutes will contain errors. **A few won't hurt you.**
Frequent or pattern errors will.

Watch specifically for:

| Error | Note |
| --- | --- |
| Subject-verb agreement | Especially with long subjects |
| Pronoun reference | Vague *this* and *it* at paragraph starts |
| Comma splices | Two independent clauses joined by a comma |
| Sentence fragments | Especially participial phrases standing alone |
| Verb tense shifts | Pick one and stay in it |
| Apostrophes | *its/it's*, plurals vs. possessives |
| Parallelism | In lists and comparisons |

The rules are in the
[grammar reference](../../sat/reading-writing/05-grammar-reference.md).

### Proofreading (final 5 minutes)

Don't reread for content — it's too late to restructure. Scan for:

1. **Incomplete sentences** — especially the last one, if you rushed
2. **Illegible words** — rewrite them clearly
3. **Missing words** — very common when writing fast
4. **Comma splices**
5. **Vague pronouns** at paragraph openings
6. **A visible thesis** — if you can't find it in the introduction in three
   seconds, add a sentence

Cross out neatly with a single line. Graders expect a draft.

## Handwriting

Underrated. An essay that can't be read can't be scored well.

- Practice **handwriting at speed** if you normally type. This is a real skill
  and it degrades fast under pressure.
- Write slightly larger than feels natural.
- Skip no lines, but keep paragraph breaks obvious — indent clearly.
- If your handwriting collapses after 20 minutes, that's a practice problem to
  fix now, not on test day.

---

## Length

There's no requirement. In practice, strong essays tend to run roughly three to
five handwritten pages, because developed reasoning takes words.

**Don't pad.** A repetitive long essay scores worse than a tight shorter one.
But don't aim short either — thin development costs you directly in the
Development and Support domain.

**Write until your argument is complete, then stop.**

## Practice protocol

| Week | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Untimed essays. Analysis only. Ignore the clock and the length. |
| 2 | Planning drills — five plans at 4 minutes each. |
| 3 | Timed essays at 40 minutes, handwritten. |
| 4 | Timed essays, then self-score against all four domains 24 hours later. |

**Self-scoring is the feedback loop.** For each essay, ask:

- **Ideas and Analysis:** Is my thesis specific? Did I analyze or summarize?
- **Development and Support:** Did every claim get reasoning and evidence?
- **Organization:** Can a reader see my structure at a glance?
- **Language Use:** Is there sentence variety? How many errors?

Score each 1-6 honestly. The domain that's consistently lowest is your practice
target.
