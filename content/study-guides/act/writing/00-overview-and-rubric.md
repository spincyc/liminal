# ACT Writing — Overview and Rubric

**1 essay · 40 minutes · OPTIONAL section · scored 2-12**

Writing is optional and separate from your Composite. It receives its own 2-12
score and combines with English and Reading into an **ELA score**.

---

## Should you take it?

**Check your specific college list.** Most colleges no longer require the essay,
but a handful still do, and requirements change year to year.

- If **even one school** on your list requires or recommends it, take it.
  Retaking the whole ACT solely for the essay is a wasted Saturday.
- If none does, you can reasonably skip it. It costs meaningfully more and adds
  40 minutes to an already long morning.
- If you're unsure of your final list, taking it is cheap insurance.

---

## The prompt structure

Every ACT Writing prompt has the same shape:

1. **A paragraph introducing an issue** — typically a debate about technology,
   education, work, public policy, or society. No specialized knowledge
   required.
2. **Three perspectives**, each two or three sentences, presenting different
   positions on the issue.
3. **The task**, which is always essentially the same:

> *Write a unified, coherent essay in which you evaluate multiple perspectives
> on [the issue]. In your essay, be sure to:*
> - *analyze and evaluate the perspectives given*
> - *state and develop your own perspective on the issue*
> - *explain the relationship between your perspective and those given*
>
> *Your perspective may be in full agreement with any of those given, in partial
> agreement, or completely different.*

**Those three bullets are the assignment.** All three must be done. An essay
that argues a position beautifully but never engages the given perspectives
cannot score well.

---

## The four scoring domains

Two readers each score the essay **1-6** in four domains. The two readers' scores
are added, giving **2-12 per domain**. The reported overall score is the average
of the four domain scores, rounded.

| Domain | What it measures |
| --- | --- |
| **Ideas and Analysis** | Quality of your thinking: the clarity of your thesis, and how well you engage the complexity of the issue and the given perspectives |
| **Development and Support** | Quality of your reasoning and evidence: are claims explained and supported, or just asserted |
| **Organization** | Structure: logical sequence, clear grouping, effective transitions |
| **Language Use and Conventions** | Sentence variety, word choice, and grammatical correctness |

### What separates the score bands

| Score | Characteristics |
| --- | --- |
| **3-4** | Minimal engagement. Position unclear or unsupported. Little or no engagement with the given perspectives. |
| **5-6** | A clear position exists but analysis is thin. Perspectives mentioned but not analyzed. Simple structure. Basic reasoning. |
| **7-8** | Clear thesis, engages the perspectives, provides relevant support, organized. **This is a solid, achievable target.** |
| **9-10** | Nuanced thesis that acknowledges complexity or tension. Analyzes the *assumptions and implications* of the perspectives, not just their claims. Well-developed reasoning. Skillful language. |
| **11-12** | All of the above, executed with precision and insight. Genuinely rare. |

### The single biggest differentiator

**Analysis versus summary.**

- A **5-6** essay *summarizes* the perspectives: "Perspective One says X."
- A **9-10** essay *analyzes* them: "Perspective One assumes that efficiency is
  the primary measure of value — an assumption that holds in manufacturing but
  breaks down when the product is education."

Analyzing means examining a perspective's **underlying assumptions**, the
**conditions under which it holds**, its **implications**, and its **blind
spots**.

If you make one improvement to your essay, make it this one. It's worth more
than length, more than vocabulary, and more than a fifth body paragraph.

## What graders actually reward

**Engaging the perspectives seriously.** Not just naming them. Explain what each
gets right, what it misses, and why.

**A clear, specific thesis.** State your position plainly and early. Do not
save it for the conclusion.

**Concrete support.** Specific examples beat abstract assertions. History,
current events, literature, science, or personal experience all count —
specificity matters more than the source.

**Acknowledging complexity.** The highest-scoring essays recognize that the
issue is genuinely difficult and that the opposing view has merit. This reads as
intellectual maturity, and it's directly rewarded in the Ideas and Analysis
domain.

**Clean, varied prose.** Not fancy prose. Correct, clear, and varied in sentence
structure.

---

## What graders do not reward

**Length alone.** A long, repetitive essay does not beat a shorter, sharper one.
That said, length correlates with score in practice because developed reasoning
takes words. Aim for substance; length follows.

**Vocabulary for its own sake.** Misused sophisticated words hurt more than plain
correct ones help.

**Formulaic filler.** "In today's modern society..." and "Throughout human
history..." are wasted sentences. Graders read thousands of these.

**A five-paragraph shell with nothing in it.** Structure is one of four domains,
and the least differentiating.

**Perfect grammar with no ideas.** Language Use is one domain of four. An
error-free essay with thin analysis scores in the middle.

---

## Practical realities

**Graders spend two to three minutes per essay.** Write so that your thesis and
structure are visible at a glance. Clear topic sentences and obvious paragraph
breaks matter.

**Handwriting must be legible.** An unreadable essay cannot be scored well. If
your handwriting degrades under speed, practice writing legibly at speed.

**You are given planning space.** Use it. Two to three minutes of planning
produces a better essay than three extra minutes of writing.

**Errors are expected.** This is a first draft written in 40 minutes and it's
graded as one. A few mistakes will not sink you. Don't stall over a word.

---

## Time budget

| Minutes | Activity |
| --- | --- |
| 0-3 | Read the prompt and all three perspectives |
| 3-7 | Plan: pick a thesis, note how it relates to each perspective, list examples |
| 7-35 | Write |
| 35-40 | Proofread |

**Planning is not optional.** Essays written without a plan wander, repeat, and
fail to connect to the perspectives. Four minutes of planning is the highest-
return time in the section.
