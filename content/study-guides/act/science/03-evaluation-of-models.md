# ACT Science — Evaluation of Models, Inferences, and Experimental Results

**Catalog domain:** Evaluation of Models, Inferences, and Experimental Results
**Skills:** Evaluate explanations · Draw conclusions · Compare viewpoints
**~30% of the section** — about 12 questions

Reasoning about whether data support a claim, and comparing competing
explanations. This domain includes the **Conflicting Viewpoints** passage, which
is unlike anything else on the ACT.

---

## Evaluate explanations

Does the evidence support the proposed explanation?

### Question forms

- "Do the results of Study 1 support the hypothesis that...?"
- "Which of the following statements is most consistent with the data in
  Figure 2?"
- "The results in Table 1 are best explained by which of the following?"
- "Which finding would most weaken the conclusion drawn in Study 2?"

### Method

1. **State the claim precisely.** Include its direction and its scope.
2. **Find the relevant data.**
3. **Check whether the data show what the claim requires** — the right
   direction, the right magnitude, the right conditions.
4. Choose accordingly.

Many of these have two-part answers: a **yes/no** and a **reason**. Both must be
right.

**The reason half is where these are lost.** A choice with the correct verdict
and a false reason is wrong. Verify the reason against the actual data.

### Patterns

**Check direction first.** If the hypothesis predicts an increase and the data
show a decrease, you're done.

**Scope matters.** A hypothesis about all temperatures isn't supported by data
from one temperature.

**"Supported" means the data are consistent with it**, not that it's proven.

**Data can be consistent with more than one explanation.** Questions asking
which is *best* supported want the one the data most directly indicate.

## Draw conclusions

Inferring beyond the data, within limits.

### Question forms

- "Based on the results, one can conclude that..."
- "The data suggest that increasing X will..."
- "Which conclusion is best supported by both studies?"

### The standard

Same as everywhere on these tests: the conclusion must be **supported by what's
shown**, not merely plausible.

**Test:** could the data be exactly as shown and this conclusion still be false?
If yes, eliminate.

### Patterns

**Conservative conclusions win.** Hedged, limited claims survive; sweeping ones
don't.

**Conclusions can't exceed the tested range.** Data from 10-50 °C support
nothing about 100 °C.

**Correlation is not causation** — the ACT tests this. Two variables changing
together doesn't establish that one causes the other, unless the design isolated
it.

**"Both studies" questions require checking both.** A conclusion supported by
Study 1 alone is wrong.

## Compare viewpoints — the Conflicting Viewpoints passage

One passage per test, typically 6-7 questions. No figures, or very few. Two or
more scientists, students, or hypotheses presenting competing explanations.

**This passage must actually be read.** There's no shortcut.

### Strategy

**Budget about 7 minutes.** Many students do it last, since it's the slowest.

**Read and summarize as you go:**

1. Read the **introduction** carefully — it establishes the phenomenon everyone
   is trying to explain, and the shared facts.
2. Read **Viewpoint 1**. Write a one-sentence summary of its core claim.
3. Read **Viewpoint 2** (and 3, if present). Same.
4. Note **where they agree** and **where they diverge**. The disagreement is
   usually about *one specific mechanism or cause*, not about everything.

That last point matters: competing viewpoints in these passages typically accept
most of the same facts and differ on interpretation. Questions frequently target
exactly that seam.

### Question types

| Type | Approach |
| --- | --- |
| **Single-viewpoint** | "According to Scientist 1..." — answer from **that** viewpoint only |
| **Comparison** | "Both scientists agree that..." — must be supported in **both** |
| **Disagreement** | "The scientists disagree about..." — find the specific point of divergence |
| **Application** | "Scientist 2 would explain X by..." — apply that scientist's stated principle |
| **New evidence** | "Which finding would support Scientist 1?" — evaluate against their claim |
| **Weakening** | "Which finding would weaken Scientist 2?" — contradict their mechanism |

### The dominant error

**Answering from the wrong viewpoint.** Distractors are systematically built
from the *other* scientist's position, which makes them feel familiar and
correct.

**Fix:** before reading the choices, say which viewpoint the question is about,
and state that viewpoint's position in your own words. Then evaluate.

### "Both agree" questions

The credited answer is usually something **modest** — a shared observation or a
premise stated in the introduction — not a shared conclusion.

Look at the introduction first. The facts everyone accepts are usually there.

### "Would support / would weaken" questions

1. Identify the viewpoint's **specific mechanism**.
2. **Support** = a finding consistent with that mechanism, especially one the
   other viewpoint can't explain.
3. **Weaken** = a finding that contradicts the mechanism or is better explained
   by the alternative.

**Accept the new finding as true.** Don't evaluate its plausibility — evaluate
its effect.

### Patterns

**Answers come from stated positions, not from real science.** If a viewpoint
says something scientifically dubious, that's still its position and questions
about it are answered accordingly.

**Watch for hedging within a viewpoint.** A scientist who says a factor
"contributes to" an effect has not claimed it's the sole cause, and choices
overstating their position are wrong.

**The introduction is load-bearing.** It contains the shared facts and the
question everyone is answering. Students who skim it lose the "both agree"
questions.

## Patterns across the domain

**State the claim before evaluating it.**

**Check the reason half of two-part answers.**

**Conclusions must not exceed the data's range, population, or conditions.**

**On Conflicting Viewpoints, name the viewpoint before reading choices.**

**Correlation ≠ causation.**

**Accept "if true" findings as given.**

## Traps

| Trap | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Wrong viewpoint** | Answer drawn from the other scientist |
| **Right verdict, false reason** | Two-part answer, second half wrong |
| **Over-generalized conclusion** | Beyond the tested range |
| **Causation from correlation** | |
| **Overstated a hedged position** | "Contributes to" read as "causes" |
| **Ignored the introduction** | Missed the shared premises |
| **Used one study for a "both studies" question** | |
| **Evaluated plausibility instead of effect** | On "if true" questions |
| **Real-world knowledge over stated position** | |

## Drill plan

| Stage | Filter | Volume and method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Hypothesis evaluation | Evaluate explanations, Medium | 25. State the claim in your own words first. |
| 2. Two-part answers | Evaluate explanations, Medium → Hard | 20. Verify the reason separately from the verdict. |
| 3. Conclusions | Draw conclusions, Medium | 25. Apply the could-it-be-false test. |
| 4. Conflicting Viewpoints | Compare viewpoints, Medium → Hard | 5 full passages. **Write one sentence per viewpoint** every time. |
| 5. Support/weaken | Compare viewpoints, Hard | 20. Name the mechanism before evaluating. |
| 6. Timed | Whole domain | 3 Conflicting Viewpoints passages at 7 min each |

Stage 4 is the priority. Conflicting Viewpoints is 6-7 questions — roughly 17%
of the section — concentrated in one passage, and the one-sentence-per-viewpoint
habit is the single fix for almost every error on it.
