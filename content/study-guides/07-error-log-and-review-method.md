# Error Log and Review Method

Reviewing mistakes is worth more per hour than any other prep activity, and it
is the activity students skip most reliably. This file explains how to do it so
that it actually changes your score.

---

## Why review beats more practice

Practice generates information. Review converts it into skill. Doing 200
questions without review produces a student who makes the same mistake 200
times, slightly faster each time.

The mechanism is specific: your errors are not random. They cluster into a small
number of recurring failure modes — usually five to ten for any given student.
Find those, fix them individually, and your score moves. Grind more questions
without identifying them, and you rehearse them.

---

## The four error types

Every miss is one of these. Classifying is the whole method, because the fixes
have nothing in common.

### 1. Content error — *"I didn't know that"*

You lacked a fact, rule, formula, or concept.

**Signal:** even with unlimited time and the answer explanation in front of you,
you'd have needed to learn something new.

**Fix:** learn it. Make a flashcard. Then do 10-15 items on that specific skill.
This is the most straightforward error type and the least common above a mid
score range.

### 2. Process error — *"I knew it but did it wrong"*

You had the knowledge but applied it incorrectly: wrong setup, misapplied rule,
skipped step, wrong order of operations, misread the graph.

**Signal:** looking at the explanation, you think "oh, I should have set it up
the other way."

**Fix:** this is about *procedure*, not knowledge. Write out the correct
procedure step by step. Then do 10 items forcing yourself to write every step.
Process errors come from doing too much in your head.

### 3. Careless error — *"I'd get it right if I did it again"*

Arithmetic slip, sign error, misread a word, answered the wrong question,
bubbled wrong, transcribed wrong.

**Signal:** you look at it and feel annoyed rather than confused.

**Fix:** change a *mechanical habit*, not your knowledge. Specifically:

- Circle what the question asks before solving
- Write every step, no mental arithmetic beyond one operation
- Rewrite the equation once before manipulating it
- Check sign and magnitude before selecting
- On the last line, reread the question stem

**This is the highest-value error type to attack.** For most mid-to-high
scorers, careless errors are the largest single bucket, and they're fixable in a
week or two. A student losing 6 points a section to carelessness is losing more
than they'd gain from a month of content study.

Do not dismiss these as "silly mistakes I won't make on the real test." You will
make *more* of them on the real test, not fewer.

### 4. Time error — *"I ran out"*

You'd have gotten it with more clock, or you rushed it because the clock was
gone.

**Signal:** it's near the end of the section, or you remember panicking.

**Fix:** [pacing work](06-timing-and-pacing.md). Also note that time errors
*disguise* other errors — a question you rushed might also have been a content
gap. When in doubt, redo it untimed and see whether you actually get it.

## The log format

Keep it simple enough that you actually maintain it. A spreadsheet, a notebook,
or a plain text file all work.

| Field | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Date | 2026-08-14 |
| Item ID | `sat-math-0312` |
| Section / Domain / Skill | SAT Math / Algebra / Systems |
| Error type | Careless |
| What I did | Solved for x, question asked for x + y |
| Why | Didn't reread the stem before selecting |
| **Rule for next time** | **Circle the requested quantity before solving** |
| Redo date | 2026-08-17 |

The bolded row is the point of the entire exercise. Everything else is
bookkeeping.

### The rule column is the deliverable

By the end of eight weeks you should have 30-60 one-sentence rules, written in
your own words, describing *your* specific failure modes. Examples of what real
entries look like:

- "When the passage has a paired structure, the answer is about the
  relationship, not either text alone."
- "Distribute the negative sign on a separate line. Every time."
- "If a transition question feels like it could be two answers, I picked
  'however' out of habit — determine direction first."
- "On unit conversion, write the units in the fraction, don't do it mentally."
- "Circle the requested quantity before solving."
- "On ACT Science, check whether the second y-axis exists before reading any
  value."

That list — not any published guide — is the single most valuable thing to read
the day before your test. It is customized to you, and it addresses errors you
have demonstrably, repeatedly made.

## How to run a review session

For each missed item:

**1. Redo it before reading the explanation.** Cover the answer. Try again with
no time pressure. This tells you which error type it was: if you get it now, it
was careless or time. If you still can't, it's content or process.

**2. Read the explanation fully**, even if you now understand. The explanation
often shows a faster method than yours.

**3. Read why the other choices are wrong.** This is where the real learning is.
The app gives per-distractor rationales for every multiple-choice item. Wrong
answers teach you the writer's toolkit — which is the subject of
[How questions are built](04-how-questions-are-built.md).

**4. Classify the error** into one of the four types.

**5. Write the one-sentence rule.**

**6. Schedule a redo** for 3 days out.

Budget **2-4 minutes per missed item.** That means a 20-question drill with 6
misses needs about 20 minutes of review. If that sounds like a lot relative to
the drilling, good — that ratio is correct.

### Also review the ones you got right

Flag any item where you:

- Guessed and got lucky
- Were down to two and picked
- Took more than double the target time
- Got it right by a method you couldn't repeat

A right answer for the wrong reason is a future wrong answer. On the real test
the same item will land differently. The app's flag feature exists for this;
use it during the drill, not after.

## Spacing: when to redo

Redo missed items on a spaced schedule. Do not redo them the same day — you'll
remember the answer rather than the method.

| Redo | When | What it tests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | 3 days later | Did the fix take at all |
| 2nd | 10 days later | Is it durable |
| 3rd | 3-4 weeks later | Is it automatic |

The app's **Missed** session mode pulls your incorrect items. Use it as the
first 10 minutes of every study session — before new material, while you're
fresh.

If you miss an item on a redo, it goes back to the start of the schedule and
you rewrite the rule. Repeated misses on the same skill mean your diagnosis was
wrong: you called it careless and it's actually a process error, or you called it
process and there's a content gap underneath.

---

## Weekly audit

Once a week, 20 minutes, look at your log in aggregate rather than item by item.

**Count the error types.**

| If your log is mostly... | Your plan for next week |
| --- | --- |
| Content | Study specific skills. This is the good case — clear target. |
| Process | Slow down and write every step. Do untimed drilling. |
| Careless | Stop studying content. Fix mechanics. Highest ROI available. |
| Time | Pacing drills, 30-second rule, checkpoint discipline. |

**Count by skill.** Which two or three skills appear most? Those are next week's
targets. Ignore skills that appear once.

**Reread your rules list.** Are you still breaking rules you wrote three weeks
ago? Those are your real problems — promote them to a short "top five" list you
read before every session.

## Reviewing a full practice test

Different from reviewing a drill, because you also learn about pacing and
stamina.

**Same day, 30 minutes:** while it's fresh, note where you felt rushed, where
you lost focus, which section drained you, whether the break helped.

**Next day, 2-3 hours:** the item-by-item review above. Yes, it takes longer
than the test did. That's correct and it's why you take a limited number of full
tests.

**Then extract three things:**

1. The two or three **skills** to drill this week
2. One **pacing adjustment**
3. One **mechanical habit** to change

Three changes. Not fifteen. A list of fifteen improvements produces zero
improvements.

## Common review mistakes

**Reading the explanation and nodding.** Understanding an explanation feels like
learning and isn't. You must reproduce the solution yourself, from scratch,
later.

**Only reviewing hard questions.** Missed easy questions are more important —
they're guaranteed points you're leaking, and the fix is usually mechanical and
fast.

**Reviewing in bulk at the end of the week.** By then you've forgotten your
reasoning, which is the most diagnostic information available. Review within 24
hours.

**Not writing anything down.** "I'll remember" is false. The act of writing the
rule is most of the benefit.

**Treating careless errors as noise.** They are your score. A student who says
"I actually knew all of these" after missing eight questions has correctly
identified their problem and then dismissed it.

---

## A minimal version

If the full system is more than you'll sustain, do this instead. It captures
most of the value:

> After every drill, for every miss, write **one sentence** naming what you'll
> do differently. Keep all the sentences in one file. Reread that file at the
> start of every session and the day before the test.

That's it. Fifteen seconds per miss. It works.
