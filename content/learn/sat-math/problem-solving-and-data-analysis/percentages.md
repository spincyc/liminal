# Percentages

Percent means "per hundred": 35% is 35/100 = 0.35. Percent questions are
part of Problem-Solving and Data Analysis, {{fact:sat-math-psda}} of the
Math section, but percent thinking also shows up in exponential models and
data questions. The arithmetic is easy; the language is slippery. Hard
questions chain changes and comparisons, read a comparison from the other
side, or set a percent of one amount equal to a percent of another.

## Percent change {#percent-change}

```
percent change = (new − old) / old × 100
```

The denominator is always the original value. That single rule prevents the
most common percent error.

> **Example.** A price rises from $80 to $92, and later falls from $92 back
> to $80. What are the two percent changes?
>
> Rise: (92 − 80)/80 = 12/80 = 0.15, a 15% increase.
>
> Fall: (80 − 92)/92 = −12/92 ≈ −0.130, about a 13.0% decrease.
>
> Same $12 change, different percents, because the originals differ.

Write every change as a multiplier. It is faster than computing the change
and adding it, and successive changes just multiply.

| Change | Multiplier |
| --- | --- |
| increase by 15% | × 1.15 |
| decrease by 15% | × 0.85 |
| increase by 100% | × 2 |
| increase by 150% | × 2.5 |
| decrease by 40% | × 0.60 |

> **Example.** A price rises 20% and then falls 20%. What is the net change?
>
> 1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96, so the final price is 96% of the original: a 4%
> decrease, not zero.

Running a change backward means dividing by the multiplier, not subtracting
the percent.

> **Example.** After a 25% increase, a price is $90. What was it before?
>
> Original × 1.25 = 90, so the original is 90 ÷ 1.25 = $72.
>
> Check: 72 × 1.25 = 90. The trap answer is $67.50, which is 90 minus 25% of
> 90.

> **Trap.** Percent against percentage points. A rate going from 20% to 25%
> rose 5 percentage points, but it rose 25 percent (5/20 = 0.25).

## Percent applications {#percent-applications}

Three basic questions:

| Question | Setup |
| --- | --- |
| What is p% of n? | (p/100) × n |
| a is what percent of b? | (a/b) × 100 |
| a is p% of what number? | a ÷ (p/100) |

Watch the small words:

| Phrase | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| increased by 30% | new = old × 1.30 |
| decreased to 30% of | new = old × 0.30 |
| 30% more than x | 1.30x |
| 30% less than x | 0.70x |
| x is 30% of y | x = 0.30y |

> **Example.** A $60 jacket is 30% off, and then 8% sales tax is added to
> the sale price. What is the final cost?
>
> Sale price: 60 × 0.70 = 42. With tax: 42 × 1.08 = 45.36.
>
> The final cost is $45.36. The order of the two multipliers doesn't matter:
> 60 × 1.08 × 0.70 is also 45.36.

> **Example.** x is 30% of y, and y is 150% of z. What percent of z is x?
>
> x = 0.30y and y = 1.50z, so x = 0.30 × 1.50z = 0.45z.
>
> x is 45% of z.

When a question gives percents but no starting amount, start with 100: the
arithmetic is clean and the final number reads directly as a percent.

> **Fails when.** The question gives a real amount you need, or part of the
> quantity doesn't scale with the percent, such as a fixed $5 fee added to a
> percent-based price. Then use the actual numbers or a variable.

### A share of a changing whole {#share-of-a-changing-whole}

A part is its share times the whole: part = share × whole. When the share
and the whole both change, multiply their multipliers to get the part's.
Going the other way, share = part ÷ whole, so divide the part's multiplier
by the whole's. Or start the whole at 100 and use actual numbers.

> **Example.** A farm's planted land grew by 25% from one year to the next,
> and the share of it planted with corn fell from 40% to 30%. By what
> percent did the land planted with corn change?
>
> Start with 100 acres: 40% of 100 = 40 acres of corn.
>
> Next year: 125 acres, and 30% of 125 = 37.5 acres of corn.
>
> Change: (37.5 − 40)/40 = −0.0625, a 6.25% decrease.
>
> With multipliers: the share was multiplied by 30/40 = 0.75 and the whole
> by 1.25, so the corn land was multiplied by 0.75 × 1.25 = 0.9375.

The other direction: if the farm's land grew 50% while its corn land grew
20%, a 40% share is multiplied by 1.2 ÷ 1.5 = 0.8 and becomes 32%. Check:
40 acres of 100 become 48 of 150, and 48/150 = 32%.

> **Trap.** Reading the share's fall from 40% to 30% as the answer. That is
> 10 percentage points, or a 25% decrease in the share, and neither is the
> change in the corn land, because the whole grew.

## What Hard looks like {#hard}

- A chain of changes or comparisons ("A is 20% more than B, which is 25% less than C"), or the change that brings an amount back. Each percent is of the amount just before it, so the multipliers multiply, and the restoring change is found by dividing.
- A comparison read the other way: "A is 25% more than B" takes the percent of B, so the same gap is a different percent of A. Give B a convenient value, such as 100, and compare directly.
- One percent of one amount equal to another percent of another: p% of x = q% of y means px = qy, so x : y = q : p, and the amount taken at the smaller percent is the larger amount.
- A share and a whole that both change. The part is share × whole, so its multiplier is the product of theirs; a new share is the part's multiplier divided by the whole's. A share that moves some percentage points is not a percent change in the part (see [a share of a changing whole](#share-of-a-changing-whole)).

> **Example.** 30% of A is equal to 20% of B. What percent of A + B is A?
>
> 0.30A = 0.20B, so 3A = 2B and A : B = 2 : 3.
>
> A is 2 of every 5 parts of the total: 2/5 = 40%.
>
> Check with A = 40 and B = 60: 30% of 40 = 12 and 20% of 60 = 12.

> **Example.** A store raises a price by 25%. What percent discount on the
> new price brings it back to the original?
>
> Start with 100: after the increase the price is 125.
>
> The discount must remove 25 from 125: 25/125 = 0.20.
>
> A 20% discount, not 25%. Check: 125 × 0.80 = 100.
