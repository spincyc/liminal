# Transitions

A Transitions question shows a short text with a blank, usually at the start
of a sentence, and four transition words or phrases: "Which choice completes
the text with the most logical transition?" The skill is part of Expression
of Ideas, {{fact:sat-rw-expression}} of the Reading and Writing section.
Every choice fits the grammar, so reading the choices "by ear" doesn't work;
the question is about logic. Hard versions connect a sentence to an idea two
sentences back, or ask for a concession-then-return that easy readers miss.

## Logical transition {#logical-transition}

1. Cover the choices.
2. Sum up the sentence before the blank in a few words.
3. Sum up the sentence after the blank.
4. Name the relationship in your own words: "the second is a result", "the second contradicts the first", "the second is an example".
5. Uncover the choices and pick the transition that means that relationship.

| Relationship | Transitions |
| --- | --- |
| addition (the next point goes the same way) | also, moreover, furthermore, in addition |
| similarity (a parallel case) | similarly, likewise |
| contrast (the next point goes against it) | however, but, yet, by contrast, conversely |
| concession then return | still, even so, nevertheless, nonetheless |
| result (the next point follows from it) | therefore, thus, consequently, as a result |
| example (an instance of it) | for example, for instance, specifically |
| restatement or emphasis | in other words, that is, indeed, in fact |
| sequence (a later step or time) | then, next, later, subsequently, finally |

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> For forty years, the Ferris wheel at the county fair ran on a diesel
> engine. In 2021, the fair replaced the engine with an electric motor
> powered by solar panels on the roofs of nearby booths. ______ the ride now
> produces no exhaust while it runs.
>
> Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?
>
> A) As a result,
>
> B) Nevertheless,
>
> C) For example,
>
> D) Similarly,
>
> Before: the diesel engine was replaced with solar-powered electric. After:
> the ride makes no exhaust. Relationship: the second is a consequence of
> the first.
>
> B signals a contrast, but nothing is being contradicted. C needs the
> second sentence to be an instance of the first; it's an effect. D needs a
> second, parallel case. A names the result. A is correct.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> Honeybees are usually described as the main pollinators of apple orchards.
> ______ a two-year survey of orchards in one valley found that wild bees,
> not honeybees, visited more than half of the apple blossoms.
>
> Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?
>
> A) Therefore,
>
> B) However,
>
> C) In other words,
>
> D) Likewise,
>
> Before: the usual view credits honeybees. After: a survey found wild bees
> did more. Relationship: the second goes against the first.
>
> A would make the survey a result of the usual description. C would make it
> a restatement, but it says something different. D would make it a parallel
> case. B marks the contrast. B is correct.

A useful signal: if the second sentence contains a limitation, a failure or
a surprise ("found that ... not", "fell", "unexpectedly"), a contrast
transition is likely.

> **Fails when.** The negative content is itself the result or an example of
> the first sentence. "The dam closed the river to salmon. ______ the salmon
> population upstream fell by half" needs "As a result", even though the
> second sentence reports a decline. Name the relationship; don't react to
> the mood of the words.

## Sentence connection {#sentence-connection}

Some transitions connect a sentence to more than the one right before it:
the next step in a process, a return to a main claim after a concession, or
an example of a claim made earlier in the paragraph. Read the whole text,
not just the two sentences around the blank.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> Critics of the city's new tram line warned that it would draw riders away
> from buses. First-year figures partly confirm the worry: bus trips on
> routes parallel to the tram fell by 8 percent. ______ total transit trips
> in the city rose by 15 percent, since many tram riders had previously
> driven.
>
> Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?
>
> A) Even so,
>
> B) Consequently,
>
> C) Specifically,
>
> D) In other words,
>
> The second sentence concedes part of the critics' point. The third returns
> to the tram's success in spite of that. Relationship: concession, then
> return.
>
> B would make the 15 percent rise a result of the bus decline, which is
> backward. C would make it a detail of the bus figures. D would make it a
> restatement, but a rise in total trips is a new point. A is correct.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> To restore the painting, conservators first photographed it under
> ultraviolet light to find earlier repairs, which glow differently from
> the original paint. ______ they removed the yellowed varnish with a mild
> solvent, working outward from the least damaged corner.
>
> Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?
>
> A) Instead,
>
> B) Next,
>
> C) For instance,
>
> D) Nonetheless,
>
> "First" sets up a sequence, and the second sentence is the following step.
> A would mean they removed the varnish in place of photographing. C would
> make varnish removal an example of photography. D implies a contrast that
> isn't there. B is correct.

Covering the choices and predicting the relationship first is the core
method for both subskills.

> **Fails when.** You can't predict because the relationship is subtle, or
> your prediction matches none of the choices. Then read each choice into
> the blank and ask what relationship it claims ("this is a result of
> that?"), and keep the one that is true of the text. Never pick a word
> because it sounds more formal.

> **Note.** On a Transitions question the choices usually share the same
> punctuation. If the punctuation around the blank changes from choice to
> choice (a semicolon in one, a comma in another), the question is testing
> sentence boundaries; see [Boundaries](learn:sat-reading-writing/standard-english-conventions/boundaries).

## What Hard looks like {#hard}

- A blank that connects to the paragraph's first claim, not the sentence just before it.
- Two choices from the same family (therefore and in other words, however and even so) where only the exact relationship decides.
- A concession followed by a return, which students often read as a plain contrast or a plain result.

No transition word is a safe guess: each one is right only when its
relationship is the one in the text. See [answer tells](learn:sat/general/answer-tells)
for why patterns in the choices are a poor substitute for naming the
relationship.
