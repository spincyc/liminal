# Inferences

An inference question asks what the text supports without saying it
outright. Most appear as a text with a blank at the end: "Which choice most
logically completes the text?" This skill belongs to Information and Ideas,
{{fact:sat-rw-information}} of the Reading and Writing section, and it is
where many students lose the most points. Hard versions spread the needed
facts across several sentences and offer choices that sound sensible but go
one step further than the text allows.

## Logical inference {#logical-inference}

The standard is "must be true", not "could be true" or "probably true". The
correct choice follows from the text with no added information. SAT
inferences are small: one careful step.

The test for each choice: could every sentence in the text be true while
this choice is false? If yes, cross it out. Use this test explicitly on
Hard questions; it is slow at first and then becomes automatic.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> Marine biologist Keiko Arai found that young sea urchins raised in tanks
> grew faster when the water contained more dissolved calcium. In the tanks,
> calcium was the only factor Arai varied; temperature and food were held
> constant. In the ocean, however, water with high calcium levels tends to be
> colder, and urchins grow more slowly in cold water.
>
> Based on the text, what can most reasonably be inferred about urchins in
> the ocean?
>
> A) Urchins in high-calcium ocean water grow faster than urchins elsewhere.
>
> B) Calcium has no effect on urchin growth outside the laboratory.
>
> C) Arai's tank results alone cannot show whether urchins in high-calcium
> ocean water grow faster than urchins elsewhere.
>
> D) Urchins prefer warm water to water that is high in calcium.
>
> A ignores the last sentence: in the ocean, high calcium comes with cold
> water, which slows growth, so the net effect is unknown. B overreaches in
> the other direction; nothing says calcium stops mattering. D is about
> preference, which the text never discusses. C must be true, because the
> two effects work against each other in the ocean and the tanks tested only
> one. C is correct.

A pattern that often holds: the correct inference is the cautious one ("may",
"not necessarily", "cannot show"), because careful claims are the ones a
short text can fully support.

> **Fails when.** The text gives enough to force a definite conclusion. If
> the text says every sample tested positive and all were from the same
> source, "the source contained the substance" is the right answer, and a
> hedged choice that says "some samples may have" understates it. Measure
> each choice against the text, not against a style. See
> [answer tells](learn:sat/general/answer-tells).

## Text completion {#text-completion}

The text ends with a blank, and you choose the ending that follows most
logically. Read the sentence just before the blank and any connecting word:
"therefore" means a conclusion, "however" a contrast, "because" a cause.
Then predict the ending in your own words before reading the choices.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> A researcher compared two groups of office workers: one using
> height-adjustable desks and one using fixed desks. After six months, the
> adjustable-desk group reported less back discomfort. However, participants
> chose their own desks, and those who picked adjustable desks were also
> more likely to exercise regularly when the study began. The researcher
> therefore concluded that ______
>
> Which choice most logically completes the text?
>
> A) adjustable desks reduce back discomfort for most office workers.
>
> B) regular exercise prevents back discomfort more effectively than
> adjustable desks do.
>
> C) the study's design does not allow a firm conclusion about the desks'
> effect on back discomfort.
>
> D) future studies should assign office workers to desks at random.
>
> Prediction: "therefore" follows a problem with the study (people chose
> their desks, and exercisers chose adjustable ones), so the conclusion must
> be a limitation.
>
> A is the causal claim the "However" sentence exists to block. B compares
> exercise with desks, which the study never did. D is a recommendation, not
> a conclusion drawn from these results. C follows directly from the stated
> problem. C is correct.

> **Trap.** The causal leap. When a text reports that two things occur
> together, a choice saying one causes the other is wrong unless the text
> establishes it, for example with random assignment.

The connecting word before the blank constrains the answer more than the
topic does, so reading it closely settles many of these questions.

> **Fails when.** There is no connecting word, or the connecting word points
> to the sentence type but not the content. "Therefore" tells you a
> conclusion is coming, but in the example above the right conclusion is
> about the study's weakness, not its result. You still need the logic of
> every sentence.

## What Hard looks like {#hard}

- The facts you need sit in separated sentences, often the first and the last, and must be combined.
- The text is written in formal, academic language about an unfamiliar field.
- Every wrong choice is plausible in the real world; only one is forced by the text. The common near misses are a causal leap, a generalization from one case to all cases, and the right idea stated too strongly.
