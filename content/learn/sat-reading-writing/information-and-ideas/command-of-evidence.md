# Command of Evidence

Command of Evidence questions give you a claim and ask which piece of
evidence supports it best: a quotation, a hypothetical finding, or data from
a table or graph. The skill is part of Information and Ideas,
{{fact:sat-rw-information}} of the Reading and Writing section. The wrong
choices are usually relevant, on-topic and true, which is exactly why they
tempt. Hard versions use claims with two parts, or data displays where a
choice reads the numbers correctly but doesn't support the claim.

## Textual evidence {#textual-evidence}

Two common stems: "Which quotation from the text most effectively
illustrates the claim?" and "Which finding, if true, would most directly
support the researcher's hypothesis?"

1. Restate the claim exactly, including every qualifier. Many claims have two parts; underline both.
2. For each choice, ask: does this show exactly this claim, both parts of it?
3. Cross out choices that are about the topic but show something next to the claim.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> Botanist Iris Mbeki hypothesizes that a desert shrub times its flowering
> to rainfall rather than to day length. She notes that in most years the
> shrub flowers in April, the month when the rains usually begin and the
> days are also growing longer.
>
> Which finding, if true, would most directly support Mbeki's hypothesis?
>
> A) In a year when the rains began in February, the shrubs flowered in
> February.
>
> B) In most years, the shrubs flowered in April.
>
> C) Shrubs grown in a greenhouse under long days flowered earlier than
> shrubs outdoors.
>
> D) The shrub's flowers attract more bees after heavy rains.
>
> The hypothesis is rain rather than day length, and in April both happen
> together, so the useful evidence must separate them. A does: early rain,
> same day length as any February, and the shrubs followed the rain. B is
> the observation that can't tell the two apart. C points toward day length,
> which weakens the hypothesis. D is about bees, not flowering time. A is
> correct.

> **Example.** A student claims that in a short story the narrator's outward
> calm hides private worry. Which quotation from the story most effectively
> illustrates the claim?
>
> A) "She folded the letter neatly and set it on the desk, as if it held
> nothing but the weather."
>
> B) "Her voice stayed even as she read the news aloud, but under the table
> her fingers twisted the hem of her sleeve."
>
> C) "The rain had not stopped for three days, and the road to town was
> flooded."
>
> D) "She had never liked receiving letters from the city."
>
> The claim has two parts: calm on the outside, worry underneath. A shows
> only the calm. C describes the setting. D gives an attitude toward letters,
> with no contrast between surface and feeling. B shows both: an even voice
> and nervous hands. B is correct.

> **Trap.** Relevance is not support. Every choice here mentions the story's
> situation; only one demonstrates the specific claim.

## Quantitative evidence {#quantitative-evidence}

Stem: "Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support
the claim?" or "... to complete the text?" Before reading the choices, read
the display's title, the column or axis labels, the units and any legend.
That takes ten seconds and prevents most errors here. Then check every
choice twice:

- Is it accurate? Does it read the data correctly?
- Does it support this claim?

A choice can be accurate but irrelevant, or relevant-sounding but
inaccurate. The correct choice passes both tests.

A school's gardening club recorded tomato yields, in kilograms per plot, in
four community gardens, with and without added compost.

| Garden | Without compost | With compost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| North | 18 | 24 |
| East | 21 | 22 |
| South | 15 | 23 |
| West | 20 | 19 |

> **Example.** Use the table above. A student writes: "Adding compost
> increased tomato yields in most, but not all, of the gardens." Which choice
> most effectively uses data from the table to support the claim?
>
> A) In the South garden, the yield rose from 15 kilograms per plot without
> compost to 23 kilograms per plot with compost.
>
> B) Yields with compost were higher than yields without compost in the
> North, East and South gardens but lower in the West garden.
>
> C) With compost, every garden produced at least 20 kilograms per plot.
>
> D) Without compost, the West garden had the highest yield of the four
> gardens.
>
> A is accurate but shows one garden, so it can't support "most, but not
> all". C is inaccurate (West produced 19) and wouldn't address the claim
> anyway. D is inaccurate (East, at 21, was highest without compost) and
> irrelevant. B is accurate, covers three gardens with an increase and one
> without, and matches both parts of the claim. B is correct.

Comparison words are precise, and choices exploit the differences:

| Phrase | What it requires |
| --- | --- |
| greater than | a comparison at one point |
| increased more | a larger change, in the same units |
| increased by a greater percentage | a larger change relative to the starting value |
| consistently higher | higher at every point, not on average |

> **Trap.** Reading the wrong row, column or series, missing a second
> vertical axis on the right of a graph, or missing a units label such as "in
> thousands". Each produces a choice that looks accurate and isn't.

## What Hard looks like {#hard}

- A two-part claim where each wrong choice supports one part.
- A display with several series, where the correct choice compares the right two.
- A claim about a trend or a percentage, with a choice that describes a single value correctly.
- A "which finding would weaken" stem, where you must reverse your usual question.
