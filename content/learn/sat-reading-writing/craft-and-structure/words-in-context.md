# Words in Context

Words in Context questions ask what a word means in a particular sentence,
or which word best fills a blank. They are part of Craft and Structure,
{{fact:sat-rw-craft}} of the Reading and Writing section, and they come
first in each module. This is a context test more than a vocabulary test:
the tested words are often common ones used in a less common sense. Hard
versions use formal texts, near-synonyms that differ in strength or tone, and
blanks whose clue sits a sentence away.

## Meaning in context {#meaning-in-context}

Stem: "As used in the text, what does the word ... most nearly mean?"

1. Cover the choices.
2. Read the whole sentence, and the one before and after it.
3. Find the clue that pins down the meaning (see the table below).
4. Put your own plain word in place of the tested word. "Limit", "cause", "good" are fine.
5. Uncover the choices, pick the closest, and read it back into the sentence.

| Clue | Signal words | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition | that is, in other words, a dash or appositive | the meaning is stated nearby |
| Contrast | but, however, unlike, whereas, rather than, despite | the word is opposite to something nearby |
| Continuation | and, moreover, similarly, indeed | the word is similar to something nearby |
| Cause and effect | because, therefore, as a result, since | the word fits a cause or a result |
| Example | for instance, such as, including | the examples define the word |

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> The review committee praised the proposal's ambition but declined to fund
> it until the authors could qualify their central claim, which reviewers
> found too sweeping to test in a single study.
>
> As used in the text, what does the word "qualify" most nearly mean?
>
> A) pass
>
> B) limit
>
> C) announce
>
> D) defend
>
> The clue is "too sweeping to test": the claim is too broad, so the authors
> must narrow it. Prediction: "narrow". A is the familiar sense of qualify (to
> pass a test or meet a standard), which doesn't fit a claim. C and D are
> things authors do with claims, but announcing or defending wouldn't fix a
> claim that is too broad. B is correct.

Many tested words have a second meaning the SAT likes: appreciate
(increase in value), arrest (stop), check (restrain), novel (new), sound
(valid), qualify (limit), compromise (weaken), reservation (doubt). When a
common word is tested, the familiar meaning is often the trap.

> **Fails when.** The familiar meaning is the one the sentence needs. Don't
> reject a choice just because it is the everyday sense: if "arrest" appears
> in a sentence about police, it means arrest. Always decide from the clue,
> then check the choice in the sentence.

Texts written in an older style use some common words in senses that have
since faded: sensible (aware), want (lack), presently (soon), nice
(precise), indifferent (mediocre). There the modern meaning is usually the
trap, and the clue in the text still decides.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> The following text is from an original letter written in a
> nineteenth-century style.
>
> I was not sensible, until your letter came, how long I had neglected our
> correspondence. I had supposed it a matter of some weeks, and I find, on
> looking at the date of your last, that it has been nearly a year. You
> will forgive me, I hope, when I tell you that the winter has been a hard
> one, and that I have thought of you far more often than I have written.
>
> As used in the text, what does the word "sensible" most nearly mean?
>
> A) reasonable
>
> B) ashamed
>
> C) hopeful
>
> D) aware
>
> The clue is the next sentence: the writer had supposed the silence lasted
> weeks and finds that it has been nearly a year. Until the letter came,
> the writer didn't know how long it had been. Prediction: "conscious".
> A is the word's usual meaning today, which says nothing about knowing how
> long. B names a feeling the writer might have, but the sentence is about
> not knowing the length of the silence. C borrows "I hope" from a later
> sentence. D is correct.

> **Fails when.** The older text uses the word in its modern sense, as it
> does most words. An older style tells you to check for a faded meaning,
> not to assume one; the clue decides.

## Precision {#precision}

Stem: "Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise
word or phrase?" All four choices may be roughly on topic; the right one
matches the text's meaning, its strength and its tone.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> Early reviews of the novelist's first book were ______: most critics
> admired its intricate structure, but several faulted its dialogue as stiff
> and its ending as rushed.
>
> Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or
> phrase?
>
> A) glowing
>
> B) mixed
>
> C) hostile
>
> D) delayed
>
> The colon says the second half explains the blank, and that half contains
> both praise and criticism. Prediction: "partly good, partly bad". A fits
> only the praise, C only the criticism, and D is about timing, which the text
> never mentions. B is correct.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> The survey's findings were ______: only 28 people responded, so the authors
> presented their results as a starting point for larger studies rather than
> as settled conclusions.
>
> Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or
> phrase?
>
> A) definitive
>
> B) provisional
>
> C) fabricated
>
> D) irrelevant
>
> The authors treat the results as a starting point, not settled, because the
> sample was small. Prediction: "tentative". A is the opposite. C accuses the
> authors of inventing data, which nothing suggests. D is too strong: the
> results still matter as a starting point. B is correct.

When a contrast word such as "but" or "although" sits near the blank, the
blank is roughly the opposite of the nearby idea, which often decides the
question quickly.

> **Fails when.** The contrast is between two other parts of the sentence,
> not between the blank and its neighbor. In the first example, "but"
> contrasts the praise with the criticism, and the blank describes both, so
> "opposite of admired" (hostile) is wrong. Find out exactly which two ideas
> the contrast word connects.

> **Trap.** The right idea at the wrong strength or tone. "Enormous" where
> the text means "noticeable", or "stingy" where the text means "careful with
> money". Match how strongly and how warmly the text puts it.

## What Hard looks like {#hard}

- Four choices that are all real, formal words, with only one matching the exact strength of the claim (suggests, demonstrates, proves).
- A clue two sentences from the blank, or a clue that is a contrast inside a contrast.
- A tested word used in a technical or older sense in a literary or historical text.

Learning more words helps, but practicing the method helps more: cover the
choices, find the clue, predict, then match. A useful set of academic words
to know well includes assert, concede, refute, corroborate, undermine,
tentative, definitive, negligible, pervasive, ambivalent, skeptical,
mitigate, exacerbate, supplant and reconcile.
