# Text Structure and Purpose

These questions ask what a text or one of its sentences does, not what it
says: its purpose, its overall structure, or the job of a single sentence.
The skill is part of Craft and Structure, {{fact:sat-rw-craft}} of the
Reading and Writing section. The most common wrong answer describes the
content accurately while getting the job wrong. Hard versions use texts whose
structure turns more than once, and choices that differ only in the verb
(illustrates, qualifies, challenges).

## Author's purpose {#author-s-purpose}

Stems: "Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?" and "Which
choice best describes the overall structure of the text?"

Map the text first, three or four words per sentence: "states old view /
gives new evidence / revises view". Then look for the choice whose verb and
object both fit. Common shapes in short texts:

- Old view, new evidence, revised view. Watch for "long assumed", "traditionally", "until recently".
- Claim, evidence, limitation.
- Problem, attempted solution, remaining difficulty.
- General principle, then a specific case.
- A description of a work of art or literature, then the author's evaluation of it.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> For decades, textbooks described the ancient river city of Halvera as
> abandoned after a single catastrophic flood. Recent excavations tell a
> slower story. Layers of silt record several smaller floods over a century,
> and houses near the river were emptied first, while those on higher ground
> stayed occupied for generations. The city, it now appears, was not deserted
> in a day but gradually given up.
>
> Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?
>
> A) To describe the methods archaeologists use to date layers of silt
>
> B) To present evidence that revises a long-held account of how a city was
> abandoned
>
> C) To argue that floods were the main threat to ancient cities
>
> D) To explain why Halvera's residents built houses on higher ground
>
> Map: old account (one flood) / new excavations / evidence of gradual loss /
> revised account.
>
> A names a detail the text only mentions; it never describes dating
> methods. C is far broader than one city. D is not explained anywhere; the
> text says the higher houses stayed occupied, not why they were built there.
> B matches the map. B is correct.

> **Trap.** The right verb with the wrong object, or the reverse. "To
> challenge a claim about floods in general" has the right kind of verb but
> the wrong scope. Check both halves of a purpose choice.

## Function of a sentence {#function-of-a-sentence}

Stem: "Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence
in the text as a whole?" Read the sentence, then the sentences before and
after it: a function is a relationship, and the neighbors are where the
relationship lives. Say the job in your own words ("it gives a case that
breaks the rule in the first sentence") before you look.

| Job | Typical wording in a choice |
| --- | --- |
| introduce a topic | introduces a phenomenon that the text goes on to explain |
| present a common view | describes a widely held assumption |
| give evidence | offers a finding that supports the preceding claim |
| give a counterexample | presents a case that complicates the claim |
| qualify a claim | acknowledges a limitation of the finding |
| conclude | states a conclusion drawn from the preceding discussion |

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> Many gardeners assume that earthworms always benefit soil. In most
> farmland, that assumption holds: worms mix fallen matter into the ground
> and open channels for water. In some northern forests, however, worms
> brought in by settlers have eaten through the thick layer of leaves that
> native wildflowers need, and those wildflowers have grown scarce. Whether a
> worm helps or harms, the research suggests, depends on the ecosystem it
> enters.
>
> Which choice best describes the function of the third sentence in the text
> as a whole?
>
> A) It presents a case that complicates a common assumption.
>
> B) It explains how worms improve farmland.
>
> C) It states the text's main conclusion.
>
> D) It describes how worms were brought to northern forests.
>
> The first sentence gives the assumption; the second agrees with it for
> farmland; the third, starting with "however", gives a place where worms do
> harm. Prediction: "a counterexample".
>
> B is the second sentence's content. C is the fourth sentence's job. D
> repeats a phrase from the third sentence but misses its role: the settlers
> are background, not the point. A is correct.

A pattern that often holds: the correct choice names a relationship
(supports, qualifies, complicates, illustrates), while wrong choices only
restate content.

> **Fails when.** The sentence really is the text's opening or its
> conclusion, where the correct choice may be "introduces the topic" or
> "states the main claim" with no relational verb. And a wrong choice can
> use a relational verb with the wrong target ("supports the first sentence"
> for a sentence that contradicts it). Check what the sentence relates to.

### A line in a poem or story {#literary-function}

In a poem or story, a line often states a view or a feeling that the rest
of the text changes: "I used to think", "At first", "She had always
believed". Its function is to set up that change, so the correct choice
names both the view and what happens to it ("states an early impression
that the ending revises"). Read to the end before choosing, and ask whether
the text keeps the line's view or turns away from it.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> The following text is from an original poem.
>
> ```
> My father's garden was nothing but straight rows:
> beans on strings, the onions spaced by hand,
> the squash kept to its corner like a dog.
> I thought it was the dullest place on earth.
> The year he moved away, I took it over,
> and by July it ran in all directions.
> This spring I came out early with a string
> and stakes, and kneeling in the cold dirt, saw
> how much of him had gone into those lines.
> ```
>
> Which choice best describes the function of the line "I thought it was
> the dullest place on earth." in the text as a whole?
>
> A) It describes how the speaker's father arranged the garden.
>
> B) It explains why the garden grew wild after the father moved away.
>
> C) It states an early judgment of the garden that the speaker's own
> gardening later changes.
>
> D) It expresses the speaker's lasting dislike of the father's strict
> habits.
>
> "I thought" puts the view in the past. The last lines show the speaker
> laying out rows with string and stakes and seeing "how much of him" they
> held, so the view has changed. A is the content of the three lines before.
> B is not in the text: the line gives an opinion, not a reason the garden
> ran wild. D treats the old view as the speaker's last word, which the
> ending overturns. C is correct.

> **Fails when.** The text never turns. Some poems hold one feeling from
> start to finish, and a story that opens "At first" can deepen the first
> view instead of reversing it. Decide from the ending, not from the words
> "used to".

## What Hard looks like {#hard}

- Texts that turn twice (a view, an objection, a reply), so the underlined sentence's job depends on which turn it belongs to.
- Literary texts where a sentence's function is to show a character's attitude or to shift the mood, or to state a view that the ending revises; a choice that takes that view at face value is the trap (see [a line in a poem or story](#literary-function)).
- Four choices built from the same verbs, where only the object distinguishes them.
