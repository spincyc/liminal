# Cross-Text Connections

Cross-Text Connections questions give two short texts on the same topic and
ask how they relate: where the authors agree, where they differ, or how one
author would respond to the other. The skill is part of Craft and Structure,
{{fact:sat-rw-craft}} of the Reading and Writing section. The pair together
stays within {{fact:sat-rw-passage-words}} words, but you must hold two
positions at once, so budget a little more time here. Hard versions pair
texts that agree on the facts and disagree about what they mean.

## Agreement {#agreement}

Stems: "Based on the texts, both authors would most likely agree with which
statement?" and "Which choice best describes a difference in how the authors
of Text 1 and Text 2 view ...?"

1. Read Text 1 and sum up its position in one sentence.
2. Read Text 2 and do the same.
3. Name the shared object: the specific thing both texts discuss.
4. Name the relationship before reading choices: full agreement, agreement on facts but not on meaning, one text limits the other, or direct disagreement.
5. For an "agree" question, the correct choice must be supported by both texts, not just one.

> **Example.** Read the texts and answer the question.
>
> Text 1: Many historians credit cheap printed almanacs with raising literacy
> among farmers in the 1700s, since an almanac was often the only book a
> rural household owned.
>
> Text 2: Shopkeepers' account books show that almanac sales rose sharply only
> after local schools opened. Almanacs, in this view, did not create new
> readers so much as serve readers the schools had already produced. Still,
> few would deny that almanacs were among the most widely owned books in
> rural homes.
>
> Based on the texts, both authors would most likely agree with which
> statement?
>
> A) Almanacs were responsible for most gains in rural literacy.
>
> B) Schools were rare in rural areas in the 1700s.
>
> C) Almanacs were common in rural households.
>
> D) Shopkeepers' records are the most reliable evidence about literacy.
>
> Text 1: almanacs raised literacy. Text 2: schools did; almanacs served
> readers. Shared ground: almanacs were widely owned.
>
> A is Text 1's claim, which Text 2 disputes. B appears in neither text. D
> goes beyond Text 2, which uses the records but never ranks them. C is
> supported by both: "often the only book" and "among the most widely owned
> books". C is correct.

A pattern that often holds: total disagreement is rarer than students
expect. More often, Text 2 accepts Text 1's facts and disputes their
meaning, or accepts the conclusion and narrows it.

> **Fails when.** Text 2 really does reject Text 1 outright ("This claim is
> simply mistaken; the data show the opposite"). Then the correct choice
> states the disagreement plainly, and a softened "partly agrees" choice is
> wrong. Decide the relationship from the texts, not from the pattern.

## Response between texts {#response-between-texts}

Stem: "Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely
respond to the claim (or finding, or study) in Text 1?" Find the principle
or evidence Text 2 actually states, and apply it to the specific claim in
Text 1. The correct choice is a direct application, not a new argument.

> **Example.** Read the texts and answer the question.
>
> Text 1: Planners often argue that adding bike lanes to a busy street slows
> car traffic. In one study of a downtown corridor, average car travel times
> rose 9 percent in the year after bike lanes were painted.
>
> Text 2: Comparing one street before and after a change can mislead. When
> researchers examined 40 streets that gained bike lanes and 40 similar
> streets that did not, travel times rose on both groups by nearly the same
> amount, reflecting a citywide growth in traffic rather than the lanes
> themselves.
>
> Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to
> the study described in Text 1?
>
> A) By agreeing that the bike lanes slowed car traffic on the downtown
> corridor
>
> B) By suggesting that the rise in travel times on the corridor may reflect a
> broader increase in traffic rather than the bike lanes
>
> C) By arguing that bike lanes make car traffic faster
>
> D) By claiming that the downtown corridor was unusually crowded compared
> with other city streets
>
> Text 2's principle: a single before-and-after comparison can't separate
> the lanes from citywide traffic growth.
>
> A reverses Text 2's view. C overstates it: Text 2 found no difference, not
> a speed-up. D invents a criticism Text 2 never makes; its point is about
> the missing comparison group. B applies Text 2's principle to Text 1's
> study. B is correct.

> **Trap.** Swapping the authors. Under time pressure it is easy to pick a
> choice that describes Text 1's view as Text 2's. After choosing, spend five
> seconds checking that each view is attached to the right text.

## What Hard looks like {#hard}

- Texts that agree on what happened and disagree on why, so both "agree" and "disagree" choices look half right.
- A Text 2 that limits Text 1 (true only in some conditions), with one choice overstating that as a rejection.
- Formal, academic language, and a question about a specific finding in Text 1 rather than its general claim.
