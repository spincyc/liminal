# Form, Structure, and Sense

These questions test grammar inside a sentence: whether a verb agrees with
its subject, whether a pronoun matches the noun it stands for, whether a
verb has the right form and tense, whether a modifier sits next to what it
describes, and how nouns form plurals and possessives. The stem is always
"Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of
Standard English?" The skill is part of Standard English Conventions,
{{fact:sat-rw-conventions}} of the Reading and Writing section. Hard versions
separate a subject from its verb with a long phrase or put the subject after
the verb.

Start every question by looking down the four choices and noting what
changes: is/are means agreement, its/their means pronouns, -ing or "to"
forms against full verbs mean verb form, apostrophes mean possessives, a
reordered phrase means modifier placement.

> **Fails when.** A choice changes two things at once (a verb's number and
> its tense), or the difference is easy to misread (its against it's).
> Settle the clearest difference first, then check the rest of each
> surviving choice.

> **Note.** The official testing points for this skill are subject-verb
> agreement, pronoun-antecedent agreement, verb finiteness, verb tense and
> aspect, subject-modifier placement, and plurals and possessives. Wordiness,
> who and whom, fewer and less, and word-choice idioms are not on that list,
> and length is never a reason to pick a choice.

## Subject-verb agreement {#subject-verb-agreement}

A verb agrees in number with its subject, not with the nearest noun. Find
the subject by crossing out what sits between it and the verb: prepositional
phrases ("of the letters"), relative clauses ("who ran the trial") and
phrases like "along with" or "as well as".

- Two subjects joined by "and" are plural.
- In "there is/are" and "Among the ... were ..." sentences, the subject comes after the verb.
- Words such as each, every, either and neither are singular.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> The collection of letters written by the explorer's crew members ______
> now kept in a climate-controlled archive at the university.
>
> Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of
> Standard English?
>
> A) are
>
> B) is
>
> C) were
>
> D) have been
>
> Cross out "of letters written by the explorer's crew members". The subject
> is "collection", which is singular. A, C and D are plural. B is correct.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> Among the objects recovered from the shipwreck ______ a brass compass and
> two sealed glass bottles.
>
> Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of
> Standard English?
>
> A) was
>
> B) is
>
> C) were
>
> D) has been
>
> The sentence is inverted: the subject is "a brass compass and two sealed
> glass bottles", which is plural. A, B and D are singular. C is correct.

## Pronoun agreement {#pronoun-agreement}

A pronoun matches the noun it refers to (its antecedent) in number. An
organization, a team or a council is singular, so it takes it and its.
Plural nouns take they, them and their. When the antecedent is several words
back, find it before choosing.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> Several maps drawn in the 1600s still survive, and because of ______
> age, conservators handle them only with gloves.
>
> Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of
> Standard English?
>
> A) its
>
> B) their
>
> C) there
>
> D) they're
>
> The age belongs to the maps, which are plural, so the possessive pronoun
> is "their". A is singular, and no singular noun in the sentence could own
> the age. C means a place, and D means "they are". B is correct.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> After three years of public hearings, the city council voted to delay the
> bridge project until ______ engineers could review the new cost estimates.
>
> Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of
> Standard English?
>
> A) its
>
> B) their
>
> C) it's
>
> D) they're
>
> The engineers belong to the council, a single body, so the pronoun is
> singular and possessive: "its". B is plural. C means "it is" and D means
> "they are". A is correct.

## Verb form {#verb-form}

Two tested ideas live here.

Finiteness. Every sentence needs a finite verb, one that can carry a tense
by itself (proposed, proposes, will propose). Verb forms ending in -ing,
"to" forms and "having" forms are not finite, so they can't be a sentence's
main verb on their own. A long description between the subject and the blank
makes this easy to miss.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> In 1932, the engineer Lidia Moreno, who had studied bridge design in three
> countries, ______ a lightweight steel arch that could be assembled without
> heavy cranes.
>
> Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of
> Standard English?
>
> A) proposing
>
> B) to propose
>
> C) having proposed
>
> D) proposed
>
> The subject is "Lidia Moreno". The clause "who had studied ..." has its own
> verb, so the main clause still needs one. A, B and C are not finite, which
> would leave the sentence without a main verb. D is correct.

Tense and aspect. Match the time frame the text sets. The past perfect (had
plus a past participle) marks an action completed before another past
action; the present perfect (has or have plus a participle) connects the
past to now.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> By the time the survey team reached the valley in May, the river ______
> its banks twice, leaving a wide plain of fresh silt.
>
> Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of
> Standard English?
>
> A) overflows
>
> B) will overflow
>
> C) had overflowed
>
> D) has overflowed
>
> "By the time the team reached" sets a past moment, and the flooding
> happened before it, so the past perfect is needed. A is present, B is
> future, and D connects to the present rather than to May. C is correct.

## Modifier placement {#modifier-placement}

A descriptive phrase at the start of a sentence describes the noun that
comes right after the comma. Read the phrase, then ask whether the next
noun is the thing it describes.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> Carved from a single block of basalt, ______
>
> Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of
> Standard English?
>
> A) archaeologists dated the statue to the fourth century.
>
> B) the statue was dated by archaeologists to the fourth century.
>
> C) the fourth century is when archaeologists dated the statue.
>
> D) dating the statue to the fourth century was done by archaeologists.
>
> The statue, not the archaeologists or a century, was carved from basalt,
> so "the statue" must come right after the comma. A says the archaeologists
> were carved. C says the century was. D puts "dating" there. B is correct.

## Parallel structure {#parallel-structure}

Parallelism as a general style rule is not one of the SAT's official
testing points, so don't spend study time on it by itself. What official
questions do test, and what looks like parallelism, is keeping verbs in the
same form and tense when several actions share one subject. That is the
verb-form rule above applied to a series.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> Volunteers at the wildlife center feed the orphaned otters, ______ each
> animal's weight every morning, and release the otters once they can hunt on
> their own.
>
> Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of
> Standard English?
>
> A) record
>
> B) recording
>
> C) to record
>
> D) recorded
>
> "Volunteers" is the subject of three actions: feed, ___, and release. All
> three need the same finite present form. B and C are not finite and can't
> share the subject this way. D shifts to the past while the other two verbs
> are present. A is correct.

## Possessives and plurals {#possessives-and-plurals}

These are official testing points (the framework calls them genitives and
plurals), so this page gives them their own section.

| Form | Rule | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| plural | add s, no apostrophe | three researchers |
| singular possessive | add 's | one researcher's notes |
| plural possessive | add an apostrophe after the s | two researchers' notes |
| irregular plural possessive | add 's | the children's books |
| possessive pronouns | no apostrophe | its, their, whose, your |
| contractions | apostrophe replaces letters | it's (it is), they're (they are), you're (you are) |

Decide two things: how many owners are there, and does anything belong to
them? "There" is a place, not a possessive.

> **Example.** Read the text and answer the question.
>
> The two ______ findings, published in the same issue of the journal,
> disagree about the age of the fossil.
>
> Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of
> Standard English?
>
> A) researcher's
>
> B) researchers'
>
> C) researchers
>
> D) researchers's
>
> "Two" means more than one researcher, and the findings belong to them, so
> the plural possessive is needed. A is singular. C is a plain plural with no
> possession. D is not a standard form. B is correct.

## What Hard looks like {#hard}

- A subject and verb separated by a long clause with its own plural noun.
- An inverted sentence, where the subject comes after the verb.
- A sentence whose only verb sits inside a relative clause, so the main clause lacks a finite verb.
- A pronoun whose antecedent is in an earlier clause, with a singular and a plural noun both nearby.
