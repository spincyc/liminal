# Modules and pacing

The SAT gives you {{fact:sat-rw-module-minutes}} minutes for each
{{fact:sat-rw-module-questions}}-question Reading and Writing module and
{{fact:sat-math-module-minutes}} minutes for each
{{fact:sat-math-module-questions}}-question Math module. That averages
{{fact:sat-rw-seconds}} per Reading and Writing question and
{{fact:sat-math-seconds}} per Math question. This page covers why the first
module of each section deserves your best work, and how to spend the clock.
For the structure itself, see [Format and scoring](learn:sat/general/format-and-scoring#structure).

## Module 1 decides the route {#module-1}

Your Module 1 performance decides whether Module 2 is the harder or the
easier version (see [adaptive modules](learn:sat/general/format-and-scoring#adaptive-modules)).
Every scored question in both modules counts toward your score, but only
Module 1 affects the route. So:

- Start sharp. Don't treat the first questions as a warm-up; if you want one, do two or three practice questions that morning at home.
- Protect the easy and medium questions. A careless miss on question 4 costs as much as a miss on question 20, and it is much cheaper to prevent.
- Check before you leave the module. Once Module 1 ends you can't return, so use spare minutes to recheck marked questions and any student-produced response.

For a student around 500 in Math, the gain is in Module 1's Easy and Medium
questions: getting nearly all of them right is what earns the harder Module
2, and it comes from knowing the content, not from speed. See the
[Math plan](learn:sat/general/math-plan).

## Pacing {#pacing}

Spend time unevenly. Easy questions should take less than the average so
that hard ones can take more.

| Math module | Target |
| --- | --- |
| first third of the module (mostly easier) | 45 to 70 seconds each |
| middle third | 90 to 110 seconds each |
| last third (mostly harder) | 2 to 2.5 minutes each |
| checkpoints | about question 6 at 9 minutes, 12 at 18, 18 at 26, done by 33 |

| Reading and Writing module | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Standard English Conventions questions | 30 to 45 seconds |
| Words in Context, Transitions | 45 to 60 seconds |
| Central Ideas, Inferences, Rhetorical Synthesis | 60 to 90 seconds |
| Command of Evidence with data, Cross-Text Connections | 75 to 120 seconds |
| checkpoints | about question 7 at 8 minutes, 14 at 16, 21 at 24, done by 30 |

These are starting points, not official numbers. Adjust them to your own
timing data after a few timed sets.

When you have no idea how to start a question after about 30 seconds, make
your best elimination-based guess, mark it for review and move on. Two
stuck minutes on one question can cost two easier ones later.

> **Fails when.** You are making real progress, just slowly. A question you
> will finish in another 30 seconds is worth finishing. The rule is for
> being stuck, not for being slow.

In Math, questions run roughly from easiest to hardest, so when time is short
the last questions are the ones to give up, not the middle ones.

> **Fails when.** You apply it to Reading and Writing. There, questions are
> grouped by domain and each group runs easy to hard, so the last questions
> of a module (Expression of Ideas) start easy again. If you are behind in
> Reading and Writing, skip within a hard group and still reach the end.

> **Fails when.** A later Math question happens to be one you know cold,
> such as a circle equation. Scan the rest of the module before you give up
> on it; ordering is by difficulty for students in general, not for you.

Some students do the Standard English Conventions block first to bank time,
then return to the reading questions. Try it in practice before using it on
the test: it helps some students and throws others off.

## Practicing pacing in Liminal {#practice-in-liminal}

- Build a set with timing on and feedback at the end, the way the real test works: 22 Math questions in 35 minutes or 27 Reading and Writing questions in 32 minutes.
- Use timed mini tests for short, focused pacing practice.
- After each timed set, look at where the time went, not only at what you missed. Questions you got right slowly are future misses under pressure.

Liminal's sets are practice approximations of a module, not official
modules, and its timing and accuracy are feedback, not a score.
