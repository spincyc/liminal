# SAT Math from lessons to unfamiliar problems

Being able to finish a problem after someone names its method is different
from choosing the method yourself. Use these two short sets to find that
gap. They mix Geometry and Trigonometry with Algebra, Advanced Math and data
questions. Half of each set is geometry so it gets attention immediately.

These are original learning exercises, not an official diagnostic or a
score estimate. Work Set A before reading the teaching below it. Save Set B
for another day after repairing the difficulties Set A reveals.

## How to attempt a set {#attempt}

Use paper and your usual calculator. Keep lessons and answer buttons closed
until you have attempted the entire set. On paper, write three things for
each problem: the quantity asked for, an equation or relationship you chose,
and your answer with units where needed. If you cannot start, write what
you know and mark the problem; do not open its answer during the set.

Work without a timer first. Record how long you spend, so you can distinguish
not knowing a method from taking too long to use one. After checking, label
each miss: setup, execution, wrong quantity, or time. A right answer without
a defensible setup still needs review. This is a learning check; its eight
questions do not establish a skill's mastery or an SAT performance band.

## Set A {#set-a}

Use the section headings to keep the sets separate; the page's problem
numbering continues through the teaching exercises. No skill labels or first
steps appear in either set. The answers include links to the lesson that
repairs each method.

> **Try it.** Triangle ABC has a right angle at C. AC = 12 and BC = 5.
> What is sin A?
>
> **Answer.** 5/13
>
> AB is across from the right angle, so AB² = 12² + 5² = 169 and AB = 13.
> Relative to A, BC is opposite. Thus sin A = BC/AB = 5/13. The ratio
> 12/13 would answer cos A instead. Repair:
> [choosing triangle sides](learn:sat-math/geometry-and-trigonometry/right-triangles-and-trigonometry#trigonometric-ratios).

> **Try it.** An amusement park charges a fixed admission fee plus the
> same price for each ride. Admission and 3 rides cost $17; admission and
> 7 rides cost $33. What is the admission fee, in dollars?
>
> **Answer.** 5
>
> Four extra rides cost 33 − 17 = 16 dollars, so each ride costs $4.
> With admission fee b, 17 = b + 3(4), giving b = 5. Check the second
> purchase: 5 + 7(4) = 33. Repair:
> [linear functions](learn:sat-math/algebra/linear-functions#intercepts).

> **Try it.** A rectangular garden is 12 meters long and 8 meters wide.
> A path 2 meters wide lies inside the garden along all four edges. What
> is the area of the path, in square meters?
>
> **Answer.** 64
>
> The whole garden has area 12 × 8 = 96. Removing a 2-meter strip from
> both ends of each dimension leaves an inner rectangle of
> (12 − 4) × (8 − 4) = 32. The path occupies 96 − 32 = 64 square meters.
> The inner area is an intermediate quantity, not the answer. Repair:
> [area](learn:sat-math/geometry-and-trigonometry/area-and-volume#area).

> **Try it.** After a price increases by 20%, the new price is $72.
> What was the original price, in dollars?
>
> **Answer.** 60
>
> The new price is 120% of the original p, so 1.20p = 72 and p = 60.
> Check: 20% of 60 is 12, and 60 + 12 = 72. Taking 20% off $72 uses
> the wrong base and gives $57.60. Repair:
> [percent change](learn:sat-math/problem-solving-and-data-analysis/percentages#percent-change).

> **Try it.** Triangles ABC and DEF are similar, with A corresponding to D,
> B to E, and C to F. AB = 6 and DE = 9. The area of triangle ABC is 20
> square units. What is the area of triangle DEF, in square units?
>
> **Answer.** 45
>
> The length factor from ABC to DEF is 9/6 = 3/2. Both a base and its
> corresponding height grow by 3/2, so area grows by (3/2)² = 9/4.
> The required area is 20 × 9/4 = 45. Multiplying only by 3/2 gives 30,
> which applies a length factor to an area. Repair:
> [similarity](learn:sat-math/geometry-and-trigonometry/lines-angles-and-triangles#similarity).

> **Try it.** The graph of y = (x − 4)² − 9 intersects the x-axis at
> two points. What is the distance between those points?
>
> **Answer.** 6
>
> An x-intercept has y = 0, so (x − 4)² = 9. Thus x − 4 = ±3,
> and the points are (1, 0) and (7, 0). Their distance is 7 − 1 = 6.
> The vertex (4, −9) is not an intercept. Repair:
> [quadratic functions](learn:sat-math/advanced-math/nonlinear-functions#quadratic-functions).

> **Try it.** A circle in the xy-plane has equation
> x² + y² − 10x + 6y = 15. What is its diameter?
>
> **Answer.** 14
>
> Complete both squares: (x − 5)² − 25 + (y + 3)² − 9 = 15, so
> (x − 5)² + (y + 3)² = 49. The radius is √49 = 7; the diameter is 14.
> Neither 49 nor 7 is the requested quantity. Repair:
> [circle equations](learn:sat-math/geometry-and-trigonometry/circles#circle-equations).

> **Try it.** For what real value of x is √(3x + 10) = x?
>
> **Answer.** 5
>
> Squaring gives 3x + 10 = x², or (x − 5)(x + 2) = 0. The candidates
> are 5 and −2. In the original equation, x = 5 gives √25 = 5, while
> x = −2 gives √4 = 2, not −2. Only 5 works. Repair:
> [radical equations](learn:sat-math/advanced-math/nonlinear-equations#radical-equations).

## Repair the decision before repeating the arithmetic {#repair}

For every miss, compare your first equation with the explanation. If the
equation was wrong or missing, more calculation practice alone will not
repair that choice. Read the linked lesson, close it, then explain aloud
which information made its method applicable. Use these contrasts to make
that explanation precise.

| Decision | Ask before calculating | Check after calculating |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Length, area or volume | What units must the answer have? Does a scale factor apply once, twice or three times? | Length scales by k, area by k² and volume by k³ for similar figures. |
| Triangle ratio or length | Which angle is named, and which side is across from the right angle? | A sine or cosine ratio for an acute angle lies between 0 and 1. Tangent can exceed 1. |
| Circle measurement | Is the given number a radius, diameter, radius squared, or an arc length? | Substituting the center and radius into standard form should recover the equation. |
| Function output or input | Am I given x, given y, or looking for the maximum or minimum? | An x-intercept has y = 0; a y-intercept has x = 0. |
| Percent change or original amount | Which quantity is 100%? | Substitute the result into the stated change. |
| Conditional probability | Which group does the word given or among restrict me to? | The denominator counts that group, not everyone. |
| Equation solution or candidate | Did I square, divide by a variable expression, or clear a denominator? | Check candidates and excluded values in the original equation. |

Two short worked comparisons show what a useful explanation sounds like.
They are teaching examples to read after Set A, not extra test questions.

> **Example.** A model doubles each length of a solid. What happens to
> its surface area, and what happens to its volume?
>
> A face uses two lengths, so its area becomes 2² = 4 times as large.
> Volume uses three lengths, so volume becomes 2³ = 8 times as large.
> Doubling a length does not mean doubling every measurement.

> **Try it.** A solid and a larger similar solid have corresponding
> lengths in the ratio 1:3. The smaller solid has surface area 14 square
> units and volume 10 cubic units. What are the larger solid's surface
> area and volume?
>
> **Answer.** 126 square units and 270 cubic units.
>
> The length factor is 3, so surface area is 14 × 3² = 126 and volume is
> 10 × 3³ = 270. The different units identify the different powers.

> **Example.** A quantity starts at 80. Adding 12 every hour gives
> L(t) = 80 + 12t. Increasing by 15% every hour gives E(t) = 80(1.15)ᵗ.
> Both equal 92 after one hour. Do they still agree after two hours?
>
> No: L(2) = 104 but E(2) = 105.8. Equal additions and equal percentage
> changes can agree once without being the same model.

> **Try it.** A machine's output is 40 units in its first hour and
> 50 units in its second hour. Two proposed models predict either
> 10 additional units each hour or 25% more units each hour. What would
> each model predict for the third hour?
>
> **Answer.** 60 units for equal additions; 62.5 units for equal percentage changes.
>
> The first model adds 10 to 50. The second multiplies 50 by 1.25. The
> first two observations fit both; a third observation can distinguish them.

> **Fails when.** You memorize a cue word instead of the relationship.
> A question mentioning a circle can ask for a length, an area, a
> coordinate or an equation. Write what the given quantities mean before
> choosing a formula.

## Set B on another day {#set-b}

Do these eight problems after repairing Set A, with the teaching and all
answers closed. They change which quantity is unknown or how the information
is presented. Remembering Set A's answer is not enough.

> **Try it.** Triangle PQR has a right angle at Q. PQ = 8 and QR = 15.
> What is tan P?
>
> **Answer.** 15/8
>
> Relative to angle P, QR is opposite and PQ is adjacent. Hence
> tan P = QR/PQ = 15/8. No hypotenuse is needed, and tangent can exceed 1.
> Repair: [trigonometric ratios](learn:sat-math/geometry-and-trigonometry/right-triangles-and-trigonometry#trigonometric-ratios).

> **Try it.** A store sells identical pens at one price and identical
> notebooks at another price. Three pens and two notebooks cost $24.
> Three pens and five notebooks cost $42. What is the price of one pen,
> in dollars?
>
> **Answer.** 4
>
> Subtracting the purchases eliminates the pens: three notebooks cost
> 42 − 24 = 18 dollars, so each notebook costs $6. Then three pens cost
> 24 − 2(6) = 12 dollars, and each pen costs $4. Repair:
> [systems](learn:sat-math/algebra/systems-of-two-linear-equations#solve-systems).

> **Try it.** A chord of a circle is 16 centimeters long. The perpendicular
> distance from the center to the chord is 6 centimeters. What is the circumference of the circle, in centimeters?
> Give the answer in terms of π.
>
> **Answer.** 20π
>
> A perpendicular from the center to a chord bisects the chord. Half the
> chord is 8 centimeters. It and the 6-centimeter perpendicular are the
> legs of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is the radius. Thus
> r² = 8² + 6² = 100, so r = 10 and the circumference is 2π(10) = 20π.
> Neither 10 nor 100 is the requested measurement.
> Repair: [circle measures](learn:sat-math/geometry-and-trigonometry/circles#circle-measures).

> **Try it.** The function f is defined by f(x) = (x + 2)(x − 6).
> What is the minimum value of f(x) for real x?
>
> **Answer.** −16
>
> The roots are −2 and 6, so the symmetry line is x = (−2 + 6)/2 = 2.
> The leading coefficient is positive, so the vertex is a minimum.
> f(2) = (4)(−4) = −16. The input 2 is not the minimum output. Equivalently,
> f(x) = (x − 2)² − 16. Repair:
> [quadratic functions](learn:sat-math/advanced-math/nonlinear-functions#quadratic-functions).

> **Try it.** Two triangles are similar. The smaller triangle's area is
> 16/25 of the larger triangle's area. A side of the larger triangle has
> length 30. What is the length of the corresponding smaller side?
>
> **Answer.** 24
>
> The length ratio is the positive square root of the area ratio:
> √(16/25) = 4/5. The smaller side is 30 × 4/5 = 24. Using 16/25 directly
> would confuse area with length. Repair:
> [similarity](learn:sat-math/geometry-and-trigonometry/lines-angles-and-triangles#similarity).

> **Try it.** Of 90 participants in an event, 54 biked and 36 walked.
> Eighteen of those who biked wore hats, as did 12 of those who walked.
> Among participants who wore hats, what fraction biked?
>
> **Answer.** 3/5
>
> The condition restricts the group to the 18 + 12 = 30 hat wearers.
> Of these, 18 biked, so the fraction is 18/30 = 3/5. The fraction 18/54
> describes hat wearers among bikers, reversing the condition. Repair:
> [conditional probability](learn:sat-math/problem-solving-and-data-analysis/probability#conditional-probability).

> **Try it.** A closed right circular cylinder has radius 3 centimeters
> and volume 72π cubic centimeters. What is its total surface area, in
> square centimeters? Give the answer in terms of π.
>
> **Answer.** 66π
>
> First 72π = π(3²)h, so h = 8. The two circular ends have combined area
> 2π(3²) = 18π. The curved surface unwraps to a rectangle of area
> (2π × 3)(8) = 48π. Total area is 18π + 48π = 66π. Repair:
> [surface area](learn:sat-math/geometry-and-trigonometry/area-and-volume#surface-area).

> **Try it.** A population is modeled by P(t) = 200(1.5)^(t/2), where
> t is the number of hours since observations began. What population
> does the model predict after 6 hours?
>
> **Answer.** 675
>
> Six hours contain three 2-hour periods, so P(6) = 200(1.5)³ = 675.
> The factor 1.5 applies every two hours, not every hour. Repair:
> [exponential functions](learn:sat-math/advanced-math/nonlinear-functions#exponential-functions).

## Check whether the repair transfers {#next-check}

Compare your setup and execution separately across the two sets. A correct
calculation from a wrong equation is still a setup problem. If you chose the
right relationship without help but made arithmetic errors, practise its
execution. If you could not choose it, return to the lesson's contrasting
examples and write why the other method does not apply.

Next, use a few unfamiliar official questions on the repaired skills, then
include those skills in a mixed set with feedback at the end. Follow the
[official-question check](learn:sat/general/math-plan#official-questions)
and [timed-module routine](learn:sat/general/math-plan#week). Keep official
questions on College Board's site; record your own error rule, not a copied
question. Success on this page or repeated versions of its problems does
not establish transfer to unseen official questions.
